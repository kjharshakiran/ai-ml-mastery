The Big Question
A machine that does nothing but guess the next word, trained on a copy of the internet — how on earth does that turn into something that writes code, explains jokes, and answers your questions? And when it doesn't know a fact, how do we let it look one up? This is the payoff. Everything you've learned since Week 2 meets here.

34.1 Transformers for images and video

We built attention for words, but nothing about it is text-specific. The Vision Transformer (ViT, 2020) made the leap with a beautifully simple trick: chop an image into a grid of small patches (say 16×16 pixels), flatten each patch into a vector, add a positional encoding (Week 33 — images have order too!), and feed the sequence of patches into a standard transformer encoder. To the transformer, a picture is just "a sentence whose words are patches." Each patch then attends to every other patch, so the model can relate a wheel in one corner to a car body in another. For video, we extend the same idea across time — patches now carry a position in space and in the sequence of frames — so attention links a moving object across frames.

🧒 Explain Like I'm 10

Cut a photo into a grid of little square stickers, like a jigsaw. Lay the stickers out in a line and pretend each sticker is a "word." Now the same machine that reads sentences can "read" the picture — every sticker gets to look at every other sticker and say "hey, my piece of road connects to your piece of car!" That's a Vision Transformer.

34.2–34.4 Seeing and reading: captioning, VQA, and commonsense

Once images and text live in the same vector space, you can build multimodal models that join them. Three classic tasks form a ladder of difficulty:

  • Visual captioning (34.2) — given an image, generate a sentence describing it ("a dog catching a frisbee on a beach"). A vision encoder turns the image into vectors; a language decoder attends to those vectors (cross-attention, Week 32) and writes the caption word by word.
  • Visual Question Answering (34.3) — given an image and a question ("What colour is the dog's collar?"), produce an answer. The model must align words in the question with regions in the image and reason about both together.
  • Visual Commonsense Reasoning (34.4) — the hardest rung: not just what is in the scene, but why. "Why is the person running?" → "Because they're trying to catch the frisbee." This demands world knowledge that goes beyond the pixels — the same knowledge a big language model soaks up from text.

The trend is unmistakable: a single transformer, fed tokens from any modality — words, image patches, audio frames — and trained to predict, can see, read, and reason in one architecture. Which brings us to the star of the show.

34.5 Large Language Models — the whole course in one machine

Strip away the mystique and an LLM is one thing: a giant decoder-only transformer (Lesson 32), trained by next-token prediction (Lesson 33) on a huge slice of the internet. That's the entire recipe. Everything impressive it does is an emergent consequence of doing that one job extremely well at enormous scale.

Let's trace a single forward pass, naming every piece you already know:

  1. Tokenise. Split the input text into tokens (sub-word chunks, Week 9). "unbelievable" → un, believ, able. Each token has an integer id.
  2. Embed. Look up each token id in an embedding table (Week 31) to get a vector, and add its positional encoding (Week 33).
  3. Attend, repeated. Push the vectors through dozens of stacked masked-self-attention blocks (Lesson 32). Each token mixes in information from every earlier token; deep layers build ever more abstract meaning.
  4. Project to the vocabulary. The final vector for the last position is multiplied by an output matrix to produce one logit per word in the vocabulary — a raw score for "how likely is this the next word?"
  5. Softmax + sample. Softmax (Week 24) turns the logits into a probability distribution over the whole vocabulary; we sample a token from it. Append that token to the input and repeat from step 1.

That loop — predict, append, predict — is all there is to text generation. The "intelligence" is entirely in the weights of those attention blocks, learned from the next-token objective.

Scaling laws & emergent abilities

Why are recent models so much more capable? Because of scaling laws: empirically, a model's loss falls in a smooth, predictable way as you increase three things together — the number of parameters, the amount of training data, and the compute spent. More of all three reliably means lower loss. The astonishing part is that beyond certain scales, abilities appear that smaller models simply did not have — multi-step arithmetic, chain-of-thought reasoning, translating rare languages. These are called emergent abilities: capabilities that were essentially absent until the model crossed a size threshold, then switched on. You never explicitly taught the model to do arithmetic; it learned it because arithmetic helps predict the next token, and at scale it finally had the capacity.

🏙️ Real-World Analogy: emergence

One ant follows a few simple rules and does nothing clever. A million ants following the same rules build bridges, farm fungus, and wage wars — behaviour no single ant "knows." Nobody programmed the colony; complex ability emerged from scale. An LLM is similar: each next-word prediction is humble, but stack billions of parameters trained on trillions of words and reasoning emerges that no one wrote down.

From predictor to assistant: instruction tuning & RLHF

A raw pre-trained LLM is a brilliant auto-complete, but it isn't naturally helpful — ask it a question and it might continue with more questions, because that's a plausible internet continuation. Two finishing steps turn it into an assistant:

  • Instruction tuning. Fine-tune (Week 33) on a curated set of (instruction → good response) pairs, teaching the model the format of being helpful: when asked something, answer it.
  • RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback). Humans rank several model answers from best to worst; we train a reward model on those rankings, then use reinforcement learning to nudge the LLM toward answers humans prefer. This is the trial-and-error, reward-driven learning you'll study properly in Week 40 — applied to make a model honest, harmless, and helpful.

34.6 Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)

An LLM only knows what was in its training data, frozen at training time. It can't cite your company's private documents and it will happily hallucinate facts it never learned. RAG fixes this by giving the model an open-book exam instead of a closed-book one. The idea reuses tools you've had since the very start of the course:

  1. Index (offline). Split your documents into chunks and run each through an embedding model (Week 31) to get a vector. Store all these vectors in a database.
  2. Retrieve. When a question arrives, embed it too, then find the chunks whose vectors are most similar to the question's vector — using cosine similarity from Week 2. These are the passages most relevant to the question.
  3. Generate. Paste those retrieved chunks into the LLM's prompt as context ("Using the following passages, answer…") and let it generate a grounded answer that quotes real sources.

RAG is how chatbots answer questions about documents written long after the model was trained, and it's the backbone of Project 5. Notice the gorgeous symmetry: the same dot-product similarity that powers attention inside the model (Week 32) also powers retrieval outside it (Week 2). It's dot products all the way down.

34.7 Sampling the next token in NumPy

Let's demystify step 5 of the loop. Given a vector of logits over the vocabulary, the code below applies a temperature, runs softmax, and samples a token. Temperature controls creativity: low temperature makes the model nearly always pick the top word (focused, repetitive); high temperature flattens the distribution (diverse, riskier).

numpy — sample the next token with temperature
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# A tiny vocabulary and the raw logits the transformer produced for the NEXT token.
vocab  = ["cat", "dog", "mat", "ran", "the"]
logits = np.array([2.5, 2.0, 1.0, 0.2, 0.5])   # higher = model thinks more likely

def softmax(x):
    x = x - x.max()                # numerical stability (Week 24)
    e = np.exp(x)
    return e / e.sum()

def sample_next(logits, temperature=1.0):
    # Temperature divides the logits BEFORE softmax:
    #   T -> 0  makes the distribution peaky (always the top word, deterministic-ish)
    #   T large makes it flat (more random / creative)
    probs = softmax(logits / temperature)
    idx   = rng.choice(len(logits), p=probs)   # sample one token id from the distribution
    return idx, probs

for T in (0.5, 1.0, 1.5):
    idx, probs = sample_next(logits, temperature=T)
    print(f"T={T}: probs={np.round(probs,2)}  ->  sampled '{vocab[idx]}'")

# A mini generation loop: predict -> append -> repeat (the heart of an LLM).
# Here we fake the model with a function that returns logits; a real LLM would
# compute them by running the transformer on the tokens so far.
def fake_model(tokens):
    return logits + rng.normal(0, 0.3, size=len(vocab))   # pretend logits

tokens = ["the"]
for _ in range(4):
    idx, _ = sample_next(fake_model(tokens), temperature=0.8)
    tokens.append(vocab[idx])      # append the sampled word, then loop
print("generated:", " ".join(tokens))

Run it: at low temperature the probability mass piles onto "cat"; at high temperature it spreads out and you'll see more variety in what gets sampled. That single rng.choice(..., p=probs) — sampling from a softmax — is the spark of "creativity" in every LLM, and the generation loop at the bottom is a toy version of exactly what you'll build in Project 4.

🔑 The one thing to remember

An LLM is a decoder-only transformer trained to predict the next token. To use it you loop: tokenise → embed → attend → softmax over the vocabulary → sample → append → repeat. RAG bolts on an open book by retrieving relevant text via embedding similarity before generating. No magic — just attention, softmax, and scale.

🔗 Connect the Dots — the whole course, in one picture

This is the payoff. Trace it: a word becomes a vector (Week 2); a learned embedding (Week 31) places similar words nearby; attention (Week 32) lets every token weigh every other with a dot product, while positional encoding (Week 33) supplies order; trained by self-supervised next-token prediction (Week 12 / Week 33) at scale, this becomes an LLM, and a final softmax (Week 24) + sample generates text. RLHF aligns it using the reinforcement learning of Week 40. RAG reuses cosine similarity (Week 2) and embeddings (Week 31) to fetch facts. You now have everything to build Project 4 (Mini-GPT) and Project 5 (RAG chatbot). Forty weeks of math, code, and intuition converge right here.

✅ Check Yourself

In one sentence, what is a Large Language Model?
An LLM is fundamentally a giant decoder-only transformer (Lesson 32) trained with the self-supervised next-token objective (Lesson 33). Its abilities are emergent consequences of doing that one job extremely well at scale — it is not a lookup table.
In the generation loop, what happens right after softmax produces a probability distribution over the vocabulary?
Softmax (Week 24) over the logits gives a distribution; we sample one token, append it to the sequence, and feed everything back in. Predict → append → repeat is the entire text-generation loop.
Which earlier-week tools does RAG's retrieval step rely on?
RAG embeds both the documents and the question (Week 31), then ranks chunks by cosine similarity (Week 2) — the same dot-product idea that powers attention inside the model. The top chunks are stuffed into the prompt so the LLM answers from real sources.
