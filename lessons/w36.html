The Big Question
Last week our generator turned random noise into some realistic image — but we had no say in which one. How do we keep the magic yet add a steering wheel: "make me a 7," "turn this sketch into a handbag," "paint a small bird with a red belly"?

36.1 Recap: the unconditional GAN

In Week 35 we built a GAN: a generator \(G\) that maps a random noise vector \(\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)\) into a fake image \(G(\mathbf{z})\), and a discriminator \(D\) that scores how real an image looks. They play the minimax game

\[ \min_{G}\;\max_{D}\;\; \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim p_{\text{data}}}\big[\log D(\mathbf{x})\big] \;+\; \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\sim p_{\mathbf{z}}}\big[\log\big(1 - D(G(\mathbf{z}))\big)\big]. \]

This works, but it's a slot machine. Pull the lever (sample a new \(\mathbf{z}\)) and you get a random face, a random digit, a random shoe — whatever the data distribution contains. You cannot order a specific one. The whole of this lesson is about adding an input that lets us say what we want.

36.2 Conditional GANs: feeding the network a label

The fix, proposed by Mirza & Osindero in 2014, is beautifully simple: give both networks an extra input \(\mathbf{y}\) — the condition. \(\mathbf{y}\) could be a class label ("the digit 7"), a one-hot vector, or later a whole text embedding. Now the generator becomes \(G(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{y})\) and the discriminator becomes \(D(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y})\), and the objective just carries \(\mathbf{y}\) along:

\[ \min_{G}\;\max_{D}\;\; \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\big[\log D(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\big] \;+\; \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}}\big[\log\big(1 - D(G(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}),\mathbf{y})\big)\big]. \]

The crucial detail is that the discriminator also sees \(\mathbf{y}\). It no longer asks merely "is this a real image?" but "is this a real image of the thing the label promised?" That forces the generator to respect the label: a gorgeous handwritten 3 submitted under the label "7" is now flagged as fake. The condition becomes a contract both players must honour.

🧒 Explain Like I'm 10

Last week the forger painted any fake money and the detective just asked "real or fake?" Now we play a stricter game: someone shouts out a number — say "five-dollar bill!" — and the forger must paint that exact bill. The detective now checks two things: does it look real, and is it actually a five? If the forger paints a perfect twenty when "five" was called, that's caught too. By forcing the order to match the picture, the forger slowly learns to paint whatever bill you ask for.

How is \(\mathbf{y}\) actually injected? The simplest way — which we'll see in code — is concatenation: turn the label into a vector (one-hot, or a learned embedding as in Week 31) and glue it onto \(\mathbf{z}\) before the first layer of \(G\). For the discriminator, you tile the label into an extra channel and stack it onto the image. The networks then learn, by backprop, how to use that side-information.

36.3 Image-to-image translation: pix2pix

Now make the condition itself an image. Instead of \(\mathbf{y}=\)"the digit 7", let \(\mathbf{y}=\) an edge sketch, and ask \(G\) to output the matching photo. This is image-to-image translation, and the landmark system is pix2pix (Isola et al., 2017). It learns mappings like sketch → handbag, map → satellite photo, black-and-white → colour, daytime → night.

pix2pix is a conditional GAN where the generator is a U-Net (an encoder–decoder with skip connections — a cousin of the segmentation networks from Week 29) that takes the input image and produces the output image. The discriminator is a PatchGAN: rather than scoring the whole image with one number, it scores many overlapping patches as real/fake, which sharpens local texture. Critically, pix2pix adds an \(L_1\) reconstruction term so the output stays faithful to the input, not just "plausibly real":

\[ \mathcal{L} = \underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{\text{cGAN}}(G,D)}_{\text{look real \& match input}} \;+\; \lambda\,\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[\lVert \mathbf{x}_{\text{target}} - G(\mathbf{x}_{\text{input}})\rVert_1\big]}_{\text{stay close to ground truth}} \]

🖍️ Real-World Analogy

pix2pix is like a colouring book where a master artist has memorised thousands of "outline → finished painting" examples. Hand them a fresh outline and they fill it in convincingly. But there's a catch: they only learned from matched pairs, each outline sitting next to its finished painting. The next idea, CycleGAN, removes exactly that catch.

36.4 Image manipulation: latent editing & unpaired CycleGAN

Latent-space editing. Recall the generator's input \(\mathbf{z}\) lives in a smooth latent space (Week 35). Researchers discovered that directions in this space often correspond to meaningful, human-interpretable attributes — a "smile" direction, an "add glasses" direction, an "older" direction. So you can take a face's latent code, nudge it along the smile vector, and the decoded image smiles. This is how face-editing apps morph age, expression, or hair: not by painting pixels, but by walking through latent space.

CycleGAN. pix2pix needs aligned pairs, which are often impossible to collect — there is no photo of the exact same horse as both a horse and a zebra. CycleGAN (Zhu et al., 2017) translates between two domains with no paired examples at all. It trains two generators, \(G:A\!\to\!B\) and \(F:B\!\to\!A\), plus a discriminator for each domain. The key is the cycle-consistency loss: if you translate a horse to a zebra and then back to a horse, you should recover the original.

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{cyc}} = \mathbb{E}\big[\lVert F(G(a)) - a \rVert_1\big] \;+\; \mathbb{E}\big[\lVert G(F(b)) - b \rVert_1\big] \]

This round-trip constraint stops the generators from inventing arbitrary content — the only way to translate and reverse losslessly is to change just the domain-specific details (stripes) while preserving the structure (pose, background).

🔑 The one thing to remember

A condition turns a GAN from a slot machine into a steering wheel. The condition can be a class label (cGAN), an input image (pix2pix, paired), a domain you want to reach (CycleGAN, unpaired via cycle-consistency), or a direction in latent space. The recipe is always the same: feed the side-information to both the generator and the discriminator.

36.5 Text-to-image: conditioning on a sentence

The most exciting condition is language. To go from "a small bird with a red belly" to a picture, we first turn the sentence into a text embedding — a dense vector that captures its meaning, exactly the kind of representation you built in Week 31 (word embeddings) and Weeks 32–34 (transformers). That embedding becomes the condition \(\mathbf{y}\). Early systems such as StackGAN and AttnGAN (2016–2018) fed a sentence embedding into a conditional GAN and generated 256×256 images of birds and flowers from captions.

So text-to-image is just a conditional GAN where \(\mathbf{y} = \text{TextEncoder}(\text{prompt})\). The generator learns to map (noise + meaning) → image; the discriminator learns to check that the image both looks real and matches the caption. This was the seed of the entire "type a sentence, get a picture" revolution — though, as we'll see next week, GANs would soon hand the crown to diffusion models.

36.6 The code: how a condition is wired in

Here is the mechanical heart of every model above — concatenating a label onto the noise (for \(G\)) and onto the image (for \(D\)). The pattern generalises directly: swap the one-hot label for a text embedding and you have the skeleton of a text-to-image generator.

pytorch — conditioning a GAN
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

NUM_CLASSES = 10      # e.g. the 10 digits 0-9
Z_DIM = 64            # size of the noise vector z
EMB_DIM = 16          # size of the learned label embedding

class ConditionalGenerator(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        # A learned embedding turns an integer label -> a dense vector (Week 31 idea)
        self.label_emb = nn.Embedding(NUM_CLASSES, EMB_DIM)
        # The first layer's input is noise CONCATENATED with the label embedding
        self.net = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(Z_DIM + EMB_DIM, 256), nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Linear(256, 28 * 28), nn.Tanh(),   # output a flat 28x28 image in [-1, 1]
        )

    def forward(self, z, labels):
        y = self.label_emb(labels)          # (batch, EMB_DIM)
        zy = torch.cat([z, y], dim=1)       # <-- the key step: glue condition onto noise
        img = self.net(zy)
        return img.view(-1, 1, 28, 28)

class ConditionalDiscriminator(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        self.label_emb = nn.Embedding(NUM_CLASSES, 28 * 28)  # one value per pixel
        self.net = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(2 * 28 * 28, 256), nn.LeakyReLU(0.2),
            nn.Linear(256, 1),                # raw score; pair with BCEWithLogitsLoss
        )

    def forward(self, img, labels):
        x = img.view(img.size(0), -1)               # flatten image -> (batch, 784)
        y = self.label_emb(labels)                  # label as an extra "channel" (batch, 784)
        xy = torch.cat([x, y], dim=1)               # D ALSO sees the label -> enforces the contract
        return self.net(xy)

# --- one tiny forward pass to show the shapes line up ---
G, D = ConditionalGenerator(), ConditionalDiscriminator()
z      = torch.randn(4, Z_DIM)                 # 4 noise vectors
labels = torch.tensor([7, 7, 3, 0])            # "make a 7, a 7, a 3, a 0"
fake   = G(z, labels)                          # -> shape (4, 1, 28, 28)
score  = D(fake, labels)                       # -> shape (4, 1): real-and-matches-label?
print(fake.shape, score.shape)                 # torch.Size([4, 1, 28, 28]) torch.Size([4, 1])

The single most important line is torch.cat([z, y], dim=1) in the generator and its mirror torch.cat([x, y], dim=1) in the discriminator. That's the entire idea of "conditioning" expressed in two lines: give both players the label.

⚠️ Watch out

If you condition the generator but forget to condition the discriminator, nothing forces the output to match the label — the generator is free to ignore y entirely and you're back to an unconditional GAN. The discriminator seeing the label is what makes the condition binding.

✅ Check Yourself

In a conditional GAN, why must the discriminator also receive the label y?
If only G sees the label, nothing penalises G for ignoring it. Feeding y to D turns the label into a binding contract: an image must be real AND match its label to pass.
What distinguishes CycleGAN from pix2pix?
pix2pix requires aligned pairs; CycleGAN drops that requirement. Two generators translate A→B and B→A, and the cycle loss demands F(G(a))≈a, which preserves structure while changing only domain details.
How does a modern text-to-image GAN condition on a prompt like "a red bird"?
The prompt is turned into a dense text embedding (the Week 31 / transformer idea), and that embedding plays the role of the condition y in an otherwise standard conditional GAN.
