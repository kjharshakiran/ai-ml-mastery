Classification I: k-NN & Evaluating Classifiers

  • Run the k-nearest-neighbours algorithm by hand and in scikit-learn, and see why it leans on the Week 2 norm.

The Big Question
If you wanted to guess whether a new email is spam, the laziest possible idea is: "find the emails most like it, and copy their label." Can something this simple actually work — and how would we ever prove a classifier is good?

1. From numbers to categories

In Weeks 13–14 we did regression: the answer was a number on a continuous line — a house price, a temperature. Classification asks a different question. The answer is a category from a fixed menu: spam or not-spam, cat / dog / horse, benign or malignant. The inputs are still a feature vector \(\mathbf{x}\) (Week 2's "everything is a vector"); only the output type changes.

Formally, a classifier learns a function \(f:\mathbb{R}^d \to \{1,2,\dots,C\}\) that maps a \(d\)-dimensional point to one of \(C\) class labels. Everything in this Part of the course — k-NN today, Bayes next week, the perceptron and SVMs after that — is a different recipe for drawing that map.

🧒 Explain Like I'm 10

Imagine a new kid joins your class and you have to guess whether they like football or chess. You look at the five kids sitting closest to them. Four play football, one plays chess — so you guess football. You just ran k-nearest-neighbours with k=5. The whole trick is "you're probably like the people near you."

2. Nearest neighbour: copy your closest friend

The simplest classifier of all stores every training point and, for a new point, finds the single closest stored point and copies its label. "Closest" needs a ruler — and we already built one in Week 2. The distance between two points is the norm of their difference, the Euclidean distance:

\[ d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}') = \lVert \mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}' \rVert_2 = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{d}(x_i - x'_i)^2} \]

That single formula — straight from Pythagoras in Week 2 — is the entire engine of nearest-neighbour methods. Change the ruler (Manhattan distance, cosine distance) and you change the behaviour, but the idea is the same: similarity = small distance.

3. k-NN: ask k friends and take a vote

Trusting one neighbour is fragile — that neighbour might be a weird outlier. So k-nearest-neighbours (k-NN) looks at the \(k\) closest points and takes a majority vote:

  1. Compute the distance from the new point to every training point.
  2. Keep the \(k\) smallest distances — the \(k\) nearest neighbours.
  3. Count their labels; the most common label wins.

There is no training step — k-NN just memorises the data and does all the work at prediction time. That makes it a "lazy learner." The one knob is \(k\): small \(k\) (e.g. 1) hugs every wiggle and overfits noise; large \(k\) smooths the boundary but can blur real structure. We pick \(k\) by cross-validation, exactly the discipline from Week 14.

🏘️ Real-World Analogy

k-NN is "you are the average of the people you stand next to." Move into a neighbourhood and a pollster guesses how you'll vote by asking your five closest neighbours. If most lean one way, that's their bet for you. No theory, no model of the world — just proximity.

scikit-learn — KNeighborsClassifier
import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import make_classification
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier
from sklearn.metrics import classification_report, confusion_matrix

# A toy 2-class dataset: 200 points, 2 informative features
X, y = make_classification(n_samples=200, n_features=2, n_redundant=0,
                           n_clusters_per_class=1, class_sep=1.2, random_state=0)

# Hold out 30% to test honestly (never grade on the training data!)
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.30, random_state=0)

# k-NN with k=5. "fit" just memorises the points — no real training.
knn = KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=5)
knn.fit(X_tr, y_tr)

pred = knn.predict(X_te)              # majority vote of 5 nearest neighbours

print(confusion_matrix(y_te, pred))  # rows = truth, cols = prediction
print(classification_report(y_te, pred, digits=2))
# classification_report prints precision, recall, f1 and accuracy per class.

4. Was it any good? The confusion matrix

A single "accuracy" number hides too much. The honest starting point is the confusion matrix: a small table that breaks predictions into four buckets for a yes/no problem.

From these four numbers we build the standard metrics:

\[ \text{Accuracy} = \frac{TP+TN}{TP+TN+FP+FN}, \quad \text{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP+FP}, \quad \text{Recall} = \frac{TP}{TP+FN} \]

  • Accuracy — fraction of all predictions that were correct. Easy, but treacherous when classes are imbalanced.
  • Precision — of everything we flagged as positive, how many really were? ("Don't cry wolf.")
  • Recall — of everything that truly was positive, how many did we catch? ("Don't miss the wolf.")
  • F1 — the harmonic mean of precision and recall, \(F_1 = \dfrac{2\,PR}{P+R}\), a single score when you need balance.

5. Precision vs recall: the disease-test story

Suppose 1 in 1000 people has a rare disease, and you build a "test" that always says healthy. Its accuracy is 99.9% — and it is utterly useless, because it never catches a single sick person. This is why accuracy alone lies.

🔑 The one thing to remember

Recall is "how many real cases did we catch?" — what a cancer screening must maximise, because a miss (false negative) is deadly. Precision is "when we sound the alarm, are we right?" — what a spam filter must protect, because a false alarm (deleting a real email) is costly. You usually can't max both; you choose which mistake hurts more.

A cancer screen accepts more false alarms (low precision) to avoid missing tumours (high recall) — flagged patients just get a follow-up test. A spam filter does the opposite: better to let a little spam through (lower recall) than to bury a job offer in the spam folder (it guards precision).

6. ROC and AUC: rating a classifier at every threshold

Most classifiers output a probability, not a hard label; you pick a threshold to convert it. Slide the threshold from strict to lenient and you trade recall against false alarms. The ROC curve plots the true-positive rate (recall) against the false-positive rate \(FP/(FP+TN)\) across every threshold. The AUC (area under the curve) squeezes the whole curve into one number: 1.0 is a perfect classifier, 0.5 is a coin flip. AUC is the threshold-free way to ask "how well does this model rank positives above negatives?"

🔗 Connect the Dots

k-NN's "closeness" is literally the Euclidean norm from Week 2 — the same ruler that powers cosine similarity and, later, attention. And the evaluation toolkit you learned here — confusion matrix, precision, recall, F1, ROC/AUC — is universal: you will grade Bayes & Naïve Bayes (Week 16), the perceptron (Week 17), SVMs (Week 18), trees, and even neural networks with these exact metrics. Naïve Bayes plus this evaluation kit is the backbone of Project 2 (the spam classifier).

✅ Check Yourself

In k-NN, what does the "k" actually control?
k is the size of the voting committee. Small k overfits to noise; large k smooths the decision boundary. k-NN has no gradient-descent training at all.
A disease affects 1 in 1000 people. A test that always predicts "healthy" achieves 99.9% accuracy. Why is it useless?
With heavy class imbalance, high accuracy can hide total failure on the rare class. Recall (caught cases ÷ real cases) exposes it: here it's zero.
You measure distance between points in k-NN using…
d(x, x') = ‖x − x'‖₂ = √Σ(xᵢ − x'ᵢ)². "Nearest" is defined by the very same norm you built in linear algebra.
