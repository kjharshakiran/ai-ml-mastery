Paradigms of ML I: Supervised & Unsupervised

⏱️ 45 min🎯 How machines learn from data📦 Week 11

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to…

  • Say precisely what "learning from data" means — and how it differs from ordinary programming.
  • Draw the family tree of ML paradigms and place any new algorithm on it.
  • Explain supervised learning and split it into classification vs regression.
  • Explain unsupervised learning and its two big jobs: clustering and dimensionality reduction.
  • Look at a real problem and instantly name which paradigm it needs.
The Big Question
A traditional programmer writes the rules by hand. But how would you write the rules for "is this email spam?" or "which customers are about to leave?" — when you can't even list the rules yourself?

1. What "learning from data" actually means

For most of computing history we did it one way: a human stares at a problem, figures out the rules, and types them as code. IF income > 50000 AND age < 30 THEN approve. This works beautifully — until the rules are too subtle, too numerous, or too tangled for any human to write down. Nobody can write the if-statements that distinguish a cat photo from a dog photo pixel by pixel.

Machine learning flips the arrow. Instead of giving the computer the rules and watching it produce answers, we give it answers (examples) and let it produce the rules itself. The "rules" it learns are called a model — a function \(f\) with adjustable knobs (parameters) that we tune until \(f\) reproduces the examples well and, crucially, generalises to examples it has never seen.

🧒 Explain Like I'm 10

Imagine teaching a friend to recognise dogs. You could try to describe a dog in words ("four legs, a tail, floppy ears…") — but a cat fits that too! Instead, you just point at hundreds of animals and say "dog… not a dog… dog…". After enough pointing, your friend just knows. That's machine learning: learning from lots of examples instead of from a written rulebook.

Formally, we have data — a pile of examples \(\{(\mathbf{x}_1,\dots),(\mathbf{x}_2,\dots),\dots\}\) where each \(\mathbf{x}_i\) is a feature vector (remember Week 2: everything becomes a vector). The whole game of Part 4 is: find a function that captures the pattern in this pile. What separates the paradigms is one simple question: what extra information comes with each example?

2. The family tree of paradigms

There are a handful of fundamentally different teaching situations, and they're organised by the kind of feedback the learner gets. This tree is the map for all of Part 4 (and most of the rest of the course):

The branching question is always the same: what does each training example come with? A correct answer attached? That's supervised. Nothing but the data? Unsupervised. A reward signal earned by acting? Reinforcement. Get this one question and the whole tree falls into place.

3. Supervised learning: learning with an answer key

In supervised learning every training example is a pair: an input \(\mathbf{x}\) and its correct output \(y\), the label. The dataset is \(\{(\mathbf{x}_1, y_1), \dots, (\mathbf{x}_n, y_n)\}\), and we want a function \(f\) such that \(f(\mathbf{x}_i) \approx y_i\) — and that keeps working on new \(\mathbf{x}\) it never saw.

📚 Real-World Analogy

Supervised learning is studying with flashcards that have the answer on the back. Question on the front (\(\mathbf{x}\)), answer on the back (\(y\)). You quiz yourself thousands of times, peeking at the back to correct yourself, until you can answer cards you've never seen. The "answer on the back" is the supervision — a teacher signal telling you exactly how wrong you were.

Supervised learning splits cleanly in two, depending on the type of label:

  • Classification — the label is a category from a fixed set. Spam / not-spam. Cat / dog / horse. Tumour benign / malignant. The output is discrete. (Algorithms in Weeks 15–18, 21–22.)
  • Regression — the label is a number on a continuous scale. Tomorrow's temperature. A house's price. A patient's blood pressure next year. The output is real-valued. (Weeks 13–14.)

How does the machine "know" it's wrong? Through a loss function that scores the gap between its prediction \(f(\mathbf{x})\) and the truth \(y\). For regression the classic is squared error; averaged over the data it becomes mean squared error:

\[ \text{MSE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(y_i - f(\mathbf{x}_i)\bigr)^2 \]

Training = nudging the model's knobs to make this number small (the optimisation idea from Week 3). That's literally all supervised learning is: guess, measure the error, adjust, repeat.

🔑 The one thing to remember

Categorical label → classification. Numeric label → regression. Both are supervised because both come with an answer key. Ask "what's on the back of the flashcard?" — a class name or a number — and you've classified the problem.

4. Unsupervised learning: finding structure with no answer key

Now remove the labels. The dataset is just \(\{\mathbf{x}_1, \dots, \mathbf{x}_n\}\) — raw vectors, no \(y\), nobody telling you the right answer. This is the situation most real data is born in: you have a million customer records and zero labels. The goal shifts from "predict the label" to "discover the hidden structure."

Two main jobs live here:

  • Clustering — group the data so that points in the same group are similar and points in different groups are different. "These 10,000 customers actually fall into 4 natural types." (k-Means & hierarchical clustering, Week 23.)
  • Dimensionality reduction — squash high-dimensional data down to a few meaningful directions, for visualisation or compression, while keeping the important variation. "Reduce these 500 gene measurements to the 2 axes that explain most of the differences." (PCA & LDA, Week 20.)
🧦 Real-World Analogy

Unsupervised learning is dumping a basket of unsorted laundry on the bed with no instructions and naturally piling socks with socks, shirts with shirts. Nobody told you the categories — you invented them by noticing similarity. Clustering is exactly that: structure you find, not structure you were handed.

Because there's no answer key, evaluating unsupervised results is genuinely harder — there's no single "accuracy" to compute. We judge clusters by how tight and well-separated they are, and dimensionality reduction by how much variation it preserves. We'll make all of this concrete in Weeks 20 and 23.

5. A tiny taste in code

Let's see both paradigms side by side on the classic Iris flowers dataset. Supervised: predict the species (a label). Unsupervised: ignore the labels and let k-Means find groups on its own.

scikit-learn — both paradigms
import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import load_iris
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split

X, y = load_iris(return_X_y=True)   # X: 150 flowers x 4 measurements, y: species 0/1/2

# --- SUPERVISED: we DO use the labels y to learn species ---
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.3, random_state=0)
clf = LogisticRegression(max_iter=500).fit(X_tr, y_tr)   # learn from answer key
print("Supervised accuracy:", round(clf.score(X_te, y_te), 3))  # ~0.98

# --- UNSUPERVISED: we IGNORE y entirely and just look for 3 groups ---
km = KMeans(n_clusters=3, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit(X)   # no y passed in!
print("Cluster labels found:", np.unique(km.labels_))         # [0 1 2] -- it invented 3 groups

# The clusters often line up with the real species -- structure was there all along
# (cluster IDs are arbitrary, so a direct == comparison isn't meaningful)

Notice the single most important difference in the whole lesson: the supervised call passes y in, the unsupervised call does not. That one argument is the entire distinction between the two great branches of the tree.

✅ Check Yourself

You're predicting a house's sale price in dollars from its features. Which paradigm and task?
You have labelled examples (past sales with known prices), so it's supervised. The label is a continuous number (price), so it's regression — not classification, which predicts categories.
A marketing team hands you a million customer records with no labels and asks "what types of customers do we have?" The natural approach is…
No labels means unsupervised. "What natural groups exist?" is the definition of clustering. The algorithm discovers the customer types rather than predicting a known answer.
What single change turns the supervised example into an unsupervised one in our Iris code?
The defining difference is the answer key. Supervised fit(X, y) uses labels; unsupervised fit(X) sees only the inputs and must find structure on its own.
