📐 Mathematical Foundations

Calculus: Derivatives & Optimisation

⏱️ 55 min🎯 The mathematics of improvement📦 Week 3

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to…

  • Read a derivative as a slope — the rate at which a function changes — and feel where it comes from (the limit).
  • Generalise the derivative of a multi-input function to the gradient: a vector that points uphill, steepest.
  • Recognise the Jacobian as the gradient's big sibling for vector-valued functions.
  • Find the minimum of a function by setting its gradient to zero (unconstrained optimisation) — and understand why this is how machines learn.
  • Get the intuition for constrained optimisation with Lagrange multipliers, and run one step of gradient descent in NumPy.
The Big Question
A model starts out terrible and full of random numbers. With no human nudging each knob, how does it figure out which way to turn every dial to get better?

0. Why are we even doing this?

Before the slopes and symbols, here's the point of all of calculus for us — and why a machine-learning course spends a week on it.

🎯 The entire point, in one line

A model learns by tweaking its numbers to make its mistakes smaller. Calculus is the tool that answers the only question that matters at each step: "if I nudge this number up a little, does my error go up or down — and how fast?" That answer is the derivative. Point every number in the direction that lowers the error, take a tiny step, repeat — and the model gets better on its own.

Picture the model's total error as a landscape of hills and valleys. A random, untrained model is standing somewhere up on a hillside. "Learning" just means walking downhill to the valley (low error). But the model is blindfolded — it can't see the valley. All it can feel is the slope under its feet: which way is down, and how steep. That slope is exactly what a derivative measures. Feeling the slope and stepping downhill is the entire training loop of every model in this course — it's called gradient descent (Week 26), and you can't understand it without the derivative.

🧒 Explain Like I'm 10

You're blindfolded on a hill and want to reach the bottom. You can't see anything, but you can feel which way your feet tilt. So you take a small step in the steepest-downhill direction, feel again, step again. Soon you're at the bottom. The "feel of the tilt" is the derivative; the walking-down is how a computer learns. Calculus is just the math of feeling the tilt.

So when the formulas below talk about slopes, gradients, and minima, remember they're all in service of one job: tell the model which way to nudge each of its numbers to be a little less wrong. Now the symbols have a purpose.

1. The derivative is just a slope

Last week vectors gave us the language of space. Calculus gives us the language of change. And the whole of single-variable calculus boils down to one honest question: if I nudge the input a tiny bit, how much does the output move?

Picture a function \(f(x)\) as a hilly road, with \(x\) the distance you've walked and \(f(x)\) your altitude. The derivative \(f'(x)\) is the steepness of the road right under your feet — positive when you're climbing, negative when you're descending, zero at the very top or bottom of a hill.

Formally, we take the slope between two points and slide them together until they almost touch — that "almost touching" is the limit:

\[ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} \]

The fraction is plain "rise over run." The limit just shrinks the run \(h\) to an infinitesimal sliver so the slope becomes exact at a single point instead of averaged over a stretch. For \(f(x)=x^2\), this machinery gives the famous \(f'(x)=2x\): at \(x=3\) the road climbs with steepness \(6\).

🧒 Explain Like I'm 10

Imagine you're on a bike. The derivative is your speedometer for the hill: it tells you how steep the ground is right where your wheels are. A big number means a steep climb (hard pedalling). A negative number means you're rolling downhill. Zero means the ground is flat — you've reached the top of a bump or the bottom of a dip. That single number — "how steep, and which way" — is everything calculus is about.

2. From one input to many: the gradient

Real models don't depend on a single \(x\). A loss might depend on millions of parameters. So we ask the same nudge question per input: "if I wiggle only \(x_1\) (holding the rest fixed), how does the output move?" That's a partial derivative, written \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}\).

Collect all the partials into a single vector and you get the gradient — the most important object in this entire course:

\[ \nabla f(\mathbf{x}) = \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_1} \\[4pt] \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_2} \\[2pt] \vdots \\[2pt] \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_n} \end{bmatrix} \]

Notice the gradient is a vector — exactly the arrows we built in Week 2. And it has a gorgeous geometric meaning: at any point, \(\nabla f\) points in the direction of steepest ascent, and its length tells you how steep that climb is. Walk along it and the function rises fastest; walk against it (\(-\nabla f\)) and it falls fastest.

🏔️ Real-World Analogy

You're standing on a foggy mountainside and want to reach the valley, but you can only see one metre around your boots. What do you do? Feel which way the ground slopes most steeply downward, take a step that way, then repeat. The "steepest downward" direction is exactly \(-\nabla f\). That blind, step-by-step descent is the algorithm that trains nearly every model you'll ever build. It's called gradient descent.

3. Vector-in, vector-out: the Jacobian

Sometimes a function takes a vector and returns a vector — say \(\mathbf{f}: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m\). One gradient isn't enough; we need the partial of every output with respect to every input. Stack them into a grid and you have the Jacobian matrix:

\[ J = \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_1} & \cdots & \dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_n} \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \dfrac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_1} & \cdots & \dfrac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_n} \end{bmatrix} \]

If a function with one output has a gradient (a vector), then a function with many outputs has a Jacobian (a matrix — each row is the gradient of one output). You won't compute these by hand often, but the name matters: when we chain layers together in backpropagation (Week 25), the chain rule multiplies Jacobians, and that's the entire engine of deep learning.

4. Unconstrained optimisation: chase the zero gradient

Here's where it all pays off. To find the minimum of a smooth function, look for the spot where it's perfectly flat — where the slope vanishes:

\[ \nabla f(\mathbf{x}^\star) = \mathbf{0} \]

At a valley floor, you can't go any lower by a tiny step in any direction, so every partial derivative is zero. (A peak is also flat, so we check the curvature — the second derivative — to be sure it's a minimum, not a maximum.) This is unconstrained optimisation: no rules limit where \(\mathbf{x}\) can go.

🔑 This is literally how ML trains

A model defines a loss function — a single number measuring how wrong it is. Training = finding the parameters that minimise that loss. We rarely solve \(\nabla f = 0\) by hand (the loss has millions of inputs). Instead we descend it step by step: compute the gradient, step a little against it, repeat. One update rule rules them all: \(\;\mathbf{x} \leftarrow \mathbf{x} - \eta\,\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\), where \(\eta\) (the learning rate) controls step size.

For our running example \(f(x)=x^2\), the gradient is \(2x\) and is zero only at \(x=0\) — the bottom of the parabola. Gradient descent should march any starting point toward \(0\). Let's watch it do exactly that in code.

numpy — numerical derivative & one gradient-descent step
import numpy as np

def f(x):
    return x**2          # our loss surface: a simple parabola, minimum at x = 0

# --- 1. Estimate the derivative numerically (the "nudge" definition) ---
# We don't need calculus by hand: just wiggle x by a tiny h and measure rise/run.
def numerical_derivative(f, x, h=1e-5):
    return (f(x + h) - f(x - h)) / (2 * h)   # symmetric difference = more accurate

x = 3.0
grad = numerical_derivative(f, x)
print(f"slope at x={x}: {grad:.4f}")   # -> 6.0000   (matches the exact answer 2x = 6)

# --- 2. Take ONE step of gradient descent: walk AGAINST the gradient ---
learning_rate = 0.1
x_new = x - learning_rate * grad
print(f"after one step: x = {x_new:.4f}")   # -> 2.4000  (moved closer to the min at 0)

# --- 3. Repeat for a few steps and watch x slide toward 0 ---
x = 3.0
for step in range(5):
    g = numerical_derivative(f, x)
    x = x - learning_rate * g
    print(f"step {step+1}: x = {x:.4f}, loss = {f(x):.4f}")
# x marches 3.0 -> 2.4 -> 1.92 -> ... shrinking toward 0, the true minimum.

That tiny loop — gradient, step, repeat — scaled up to millions of parameters, is how every neural network on Earth learns. Everything else is engineering around this idea.

5. Constrained optimisation: minimising with rules

Often you must minimise something subject to a constraint — "find the cheapest route, but you must pass through this checkpoint," or "minimise error, but keep the weights on the unit circle." Now you can't just chase \(\nabla f = 0\); the unconstrained minimum might be off-limits.

The trick — Lagrange multipliers — has a beautiful geometric heart. To minimise \(f(\mathbf{x})\) subject to \(g(\mathbf{x}) = 0\), notice that at the best allowed point, the contour line of \(f\) just grazes the constraint curve. Where they touch, their gradients point the same way (one is a scaled copy of the other):

\[ \nabla f(\mathbf{x}^\star) = \lambda\,\nabla g(\mathbf{x}^\star) \]

The scalar \(\lambda\) is the Lagrange multiplier. Intuitively, if the gradients didn't line up, you could still slide along the constraint to lower \(f\) — so you're not at the optimum yet. This idea reappears in support-vector machines (Week 18) and anywhere we balance a goal against a rule.

⚠️ Watch out: local minima & the learning rate

Gradient descent only knows about the slope right under its feet, so on a bumpy surface it can settle into a local minimum that isn't the global best. And the learning rate \(\eta\) is a Goldilocks dial: too small and training crawls; too large and you leap clean over the valley and diverge. Tuning \(\eta\) and escaping bad minima is what Week 26's adaptive optimisers (like Adam) are built to handle.

✅ Check Yourself

What does the derivative \(f'(x)\) of a single-variable function represent?
The derivative is "rise over run" in the limit — the instantaneous steepness of the function. Positive means climbing, negative means descending, zero means flat (a peak or valley).
The gradient \(\nabla f\) of a multi-input function points in the direction of…
The gradient points uphill, steepest. To minimise a loss we walk the opposite way: \(\mathbf{x} \leftarrow \mathbf{x} - \eta\,\nabla f\). That single rule is gradient descent.
Why do we set the gradient to zero to find a minimum?
A zero gradient means a flat (stationary) point. At a minimum you can't decrease \(f\) by stepping any direction, so all partials vanish. We then check curvature to confirm it's a minimum, not a maximum or saddle.
