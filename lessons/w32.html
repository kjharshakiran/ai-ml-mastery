The Big Question
When you read "the trophy didn't fit in the suitcase because it was too big," your brain instantly knows it = trophy. How can a machine learn to look back across a whole sentence and decide which words matter — all at once, in parallel?

32.1 Why attention replaced RNNs

In Week 30 we met RNNs and LSTMs: they read a sequence one token at a time, squeezing the entire past into a single fixed-size hidden state. In Week 31 we used that to build seq2seq translation — and we hit a wall. Two walls, actually:

  • The bottleneck. An encoder RNN must cram a 50-word sentence into one vector before the decoder sees a single word. Information from word 3 gets diluted by the time we reach word 50.
  • No parallelism. Because step \(t\) needs the hidden state from step \(t-1\), you cannot compute the sequence in parallel. On a GPU built for thousands of simultaneous multiplications, that's tragic.

In 2017 a Google paper with a famous title — "Attention Is All You Need" — threw the RNN away entirely. Its claim: if every token can directly look at every other token and decide how much to care, you don't need recurrence at all. Every position is computed at the same time (parallel!), and any word can reach any other word in a single step (no bottleneck!). That mechanism is self-attention, and it is the entire reason ChatGPT exists.

🧒 Explain Like I'm 10

Imagine doing a group jigsaw puzzle. An old-school RNN passes one puzzle piece down a line of kids, whispering "remember what came before!" — by the end, the last kid has forgotten the first piece. Attention instead lets every kid look at the whole table at once and shout "hey, that piece over there connects to mine!" Everyone works at the same time, and nobody forgets the far-away pieces.

32.2 Scaled dot-product attention, derived

Here is the single most important idea in modern AI. We'll build it from a librarian analogy and never let go.

📚 Real-World Analogy: the library search

You walk into a library with a question — your Query. Every book has a label on its spine — its Key. You compare your query to every key to see how relevant each book is. Then you don't grab the label; you read the book's actual contents — its Value — taking more from the relevant books and less from the irrelevant ones. Attention is exactly this: query vs keys → relevance → fetch a blend of values.

Every token (every word) produces three vectors by multiplying its embedding by three learned weight matrices \(W_Q, W_K, W_V\):

  • Query \(\mathbf{q}\) — "what am I looking for?"
  • Key \(\mathbf{k}\) — "what do I offer / what am I about?"
  • Value \(\mathbf{v}\) — "the actual content I'll hand over if you pick me."

Step 1 — score relevance with a dot product. To find how relevant token \(j\) is to token \(i\), we take the dot product \(\mathbf{q}_i \cdot \mathbf{k}_j\). Remember Week 2: the dot product measures similarity / alignment. A big score means "this key answers my query well."

Step 2 — scale. When the vectors have dimension \(d_k\), dot products grow large and push softmax into a region where gradients vanish. We divide by \(\sqrt{d_k}\) to keep the scores civilised.

Step 3 — softmax into weights. Straight from Week 24: softmax turns the row of scores into positive weights that sum to 1 — a probability distribution over "how much attention to pay to each token."

Step 4 — blend the values. The output for token \(i\) is the weighted sum of every token's Value vector. Relevant tokens contribute a lot; irrelevant ones barely register. Stacking all tokens into matrices \(Q, K, V\), the whole thing is one elegant formula:

\[ \mathrm{Attention}(Q,K,V) = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left( \frac{Q K^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}} \right) V \]

Notice the beautiful re-use: the Q·Kᵀ is just a giant matrix of dot products — Week 2's similarity measure applied to every pair of tokens at once — and the softmax is exactly the normaliser you learned in Week 24. Attention isn't a new kind of math; it's two old friends arranged perfectly.

🔑 The one thing to remember

Attention = "each token looks at every other token, scores relevance with a dot product, and pulls in a weighted blend of their values." That's it. Everything else — multi-head, encoders, decoders, GPT — is this idea, repeated and stacked.

32.3 Multi-head attention

One attention computation can only learn one kind of relationship at a time. But language is rich: in "the cat that chased the mouse was hungry," one relationship is grammatical (subject ↔ verb: cat ↔ was), another is referential (cat ↔ chased), another is about adjectives. Forcing a single attention to capture all of these is asking too much.

Multi-head attention runs several attention computations — heads — in parallel, each with its own learned \(W_Q, W_K, W_V\). Each head projects tokens into a smaller subspace and attends independently, so different heads can specialise: one tracks subjects, one tracks nearby words, one tracks long-range references. We then concatenate the heads' outputs and mix them with a final projection \(W_O\):

\[ \mathrm{MultiHead}(Q,K,V) = \mathrm{Concat}(\text{head}_1,\dots,\text{head}_h)\,W_O, \quad \text{head}_i = \mathrm{Attention}(QW_i^Q, KW_i^K, VW_i^V) \]

32.4 The encoder block

A single attention layer is powerful but shallow. Real transformers stack blocks. The encoder block (used by BERT, and by the encoder half of translation models) has two sub-layers, each wrapped in tricks you already know:

  1. Multi-head self-attention — tokens exchange information.
  2. A position-wise feed-forward network — a small MLP applied to each token independently (expand, ReLU, project back), giving the model non-linear muscle.

Around each sub-layer we wrap a residual connection (Week 29 — ResNets: add the input back so gradients flow through deep stacks) followed by layer normalisation (Week 27 — normalise activations to stabilise training). The pattern is: x = LayerNorm(x + SubLayer(x)). Stack \(N\) of these (the original paper used 6; GPT-3 used 96) and you have a deep transformer.

⚠️ Watch out

Self-attention is permutation-invariant — shuffle the input words and the math gives the same answer! It has no built-in sense of order. That's a real problem ("dog bites man" ≠ "man bites dog"), and we fix it next lesson with positional encoding. Keep this gap in mind.

32.5 The decoder block

To generate text (translate, or write like GPT) we need a decoder block. It looks like the encoder block but adds two twists:

  • Masked self-attention. When predicting word 5, the model must not peek at words 6, 7, 8 — that would be cheating, since at generation time they don't exist yet. We mask future positions by setting their pre-softmax scores to \(-\infty\), so their softmax weight becomes 0. The decoder can only look backward.
  • Cross-attention. In an encoder–decoder model the decoder also attends to the encoder's output: the Queries come from the decoder (what am I writing?) while the Keys and Values come from the encoder (what did the source sentence say?). This is precisely the cure for the seq2seq bottleneck of Week 31 — the decoder can look directly at any source word.

GPT-style models use only the decoder stack with masked self-attention — no encoder, no cross-attention — and that turns out to be all you need for a language model. We'll meet that beast in Lesson 34.

32.6 Attention in NumPy — a tiny runnable example

Let's make it concrete. Three tokens, made-up embeddings, and we'll compute attention by hand with NumPy. The code prints the attention weights (each row sums to 1) and the blended outputs.

numpy — scaled dot-product attention
import numpy as np

def softmax(x, axis=-1):
    # Numerically stable softmax (Week 24): subtract the max before exp
    x = x - x.max(axis=axis, keepdims=True)
    e = np.exp(x)
    return e / e.sum(axis=axis, keepdims=True)

def scaled_dot_product_attention(Q, K, V, mask=None):
    d_k = Q.shape[-1]
    scores = Q @ K.T / np.sqrt(d_k)        # (n, n) similarity matrix (Week 2 dot products)
    if mask is not None:
        scores = np.where(mask, scores, -1e9)   # block future tokens -> ~ -inf
    weights = softmax(scores, axis=-1)     # each row sums to 1 (Week 24 softmax)
    return weights @ V, weights            # weighted blend of the Values

# 3 tokens, embedding dim 4. In a real model Q,K,V come from learned W_Q,W_K,W_V.
np.random.seed(0)
X = np.random.randn(3, 4)                  # token embeddings
W_Q, W_K, W_V = (np.random.randn(4, 4) for _ in range(3))
Q, K, V = X @ W_Q, X @ W_K, X @ W_V

out, attn = scaled_dot_product_attention(Q, K, V)
print("attention weights (rows sum to 1):\n", np.round(attn, 3))
print("row sums:", np.round(attn.sum(axis=1), 3))   # -> [1. 1. 1.]
print("output shape:", out.shape)                    # -> (3, 4)

# Causal mask for a DECODER: token i may only see tokens <= i
causal = np.tril(np.ones((3, 3), dtype=bool))
out_m, attn_m = scaled_dot_product_attention(Q, K, V, mask=causal)
print("masked weights (upper triangle is 0):\n", np.round(attn_m, 3))

Run it: the first weight matrix is full (every token sees every token), while the masked one is lower-triangular — proof that token 0 can only attend to itself, token 1 to {0,1}, and so on. That single np.tril is the difference between BERT and GPT.

🔗 Connect the Dots

Attention is the course converging. The Q·Kᵀ score is the dot-product similarity from Week 2; the weighting is the softmax of Week 24; the encoder block is held together by residual connections (Week 29) and layer norm (Week 27); and cross-attention is the fix for the seq2seq bottleneck of Week 31. ✅ Check Yourself

In attention, what does the dot product \(\mathbf{q}_i \cdot \mathbf{k}_j\) actually compute?
Just like Week 2, the dot product measures alignment. A high score means key j answers query i well, so token j will get a large softmax weight and contribute more of its Value.
Why divide the scores by \(\sqrt{d_k}\) before the softmax?
With dimension \(d_k\), raw dot products grow roughly like \(\sqrt{d_k}\). Dividing by \(\sqrt{d_k}\) keeps the softmax inputs in a sensible range so training stays stable.
What does the causal mask in a decoder accomplish?
Setting future scores to \(-\infty\) makes their softmax weight 0. The model predicts word t using only words ≤ t — exactly the condition under which it will generate text at inference time.
