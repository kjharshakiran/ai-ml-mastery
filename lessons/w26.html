The Big Question
Backprop (Week 25) hands us the gradient — the downhill direction. But the loss surface of a real network is a wild, million-dimensional landscape of valleys and ravines. How do we walk down it quickly without overshooting, stalling, or getting stuck?

26.1 Why this matters: training an image classifier

Picture training a network to tell cats from dogs. It has millions of weights, and the loss — averaged over thousands of images — is a single number sitting somewhere on a vast, bumpy surface above those millions of weight-dimensions. Backpropagation (Week 25) tells us the slope at our current spot. But knowing the slope is not enough: take steps that are too big and you ricochet across the valley; too small and training takes weeks. The art of the optimiser — how exactly we turn gradients into weight updates — is what makes deep learning practical rather than merely possible.

🧒 Explain Like I'm 10

You're blindfolded on a hill and want to reach the bottom. You can feel which way is downhill with your feet (that's the gradient). If you take giant leaps you might fly past the bottom and back up the other side. If you shuffle tiny steps you'll be there next year. The clever trick: build up momentum like a rolling ball, so you speed through the long gentle slopes and don't get jiggled to a stop by every little bump. That rolling ball is what modern optimisers add.

26.2 Gradient descent, in depth

The core update hasn't changed since Week 3. With loss \(L\), weights \(\theta\), gradient \(\nabla L(\theta)\) (delivered by backprop), and learning rate \(\eta\):

\[ \theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta\,\nabla L(\theta_t) \]

Step against the gradient (downhill) by an amount controlled by \(\eta\). Three things make this subtle in practice:

The learning rate \(\eta\). This is the single most important hyperparameter. Too large and the loss oscillates or explodes — you leap over the valley. Too small and you crawl, wasting compute and risking getting stuck. The sweet spot is often found by trying a few values on a log scale (0.1, 0.01, 0.001).

The loss surface & local minima. For a simple model the surface is a clean bowl with one bottom (convex). Deep networks are non-convex: the surface has many dips, saddle points (flat in some directions, sloped in others), and long narrow ravines. Happily, in very high dimensions most "bad" stuck points turn out to be saddles, not true traps, and good optimisers slide off them. Different starting weights can lead to different — but usually similarly good — solutions, which is why we rarely worry about finding the global minimum; any of the many good valleys will do. The real enemies in practice are not local minima but ravines (where plain gradient descent zig-zags, as below) and plateaus (where the gradient is nearly flat and progress stalls). The optimisers in §26.3 are designed precisely to power through both.

Batch vs. stochastic (recap, Week 25). Full-batch gradient descent gives the exact slope but is slow per step. Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) estimates the slope from one example (or a mini-batch) — noisier, but far more steps per second, and the noise itself helps jiggle the optimiser out of shallow dips. Mini-batch SGD is the workhorse.

⛷️ Real-World Analogy

A skier in a long, narrow gully wants to reach the bottom. Plain gradient descent points straight at the steepest wall, so the skier zig-zags wall-to-wall and barely moves forward down the gully. Add momentum and the skier builds speed along the gully, while the side-to-side wobbles cancel out — they shoot down the centre line. That's exactly the geometry where momentum shines.

26.3 Adaptive learning-rate methods

Plain SGD uses one fixed \(\eta\) for every weight and every step. The three classic upgrades fix that.

Momentum. Keep a running "velocity" \(\mathbf{v}\) that accumulates past gradients, and step with the velocity instead of the raw gradient. A friction term \(\beta\) (≈ 0.9) decays old gradients:

\[ \mathbf{v}_{t+1} = \beta\,\mathbf{v}_t + \nabla L(\theta_t), \qquad \theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta\,\mathbf{v}_{t+1} \]

Consistent gradient directions add up (acceleration); oscillating directions cancel (damping). Momentum is the ball rolling downhill.

Momentum vs plain SGD, two steps by hand

The velocity equation is easiest to trust when you race the two optimisers for just two steps on the ravine \(L=x^2+10y^2\), whose gradient is \([2x,\,20y]\). Both start at \((9,1)\) with \(\eta=0.02\); momentum uses \(\beta=0.9\) and \(\mathbf{v}_0=0\).

Step 1 (identical). Gradient at \((9,1)\) is \([18,20]\). SGD steps to \([9,1]-0.02[18,20]=(8.64,0.60)\). Momentum sets \(\mathbf{v}_1=0.9\cdot 0+[18,20]=[18,20]\) and lands in the same place.

Step 2 (they diverge). The new gradient is \([2\cdot 8.64,\,20\cdot 0.60]=[17.28,12]\). Plain SGD just steps again: \((8.29,\,0.36)\). Momentum instead accumulates: \(\mathbf{v}_2=0.9[18,20]+[17.28,12]=[33.48,30]\), so \(\theta=(8.64,0.60)-0.02[33.48,30]=(7.97,\,\mathbf{0.00})\).

After only two steps momentum has driven \(y\) to 0.00 while SGD is still stuck at 0.36. The \(y\)-gradient pointed the same way both times, so momentum added the two pushes and killed the steep-axis zig-zag — exactly the ravine win the skier analogy promised.

⚠️ Velocity can overshoot

That same accumulation is double-edged: a velocity built up over many consistent steps can carry you past the minimum before it reverses, so the loss may briefly tick up. The friction term \(\beta\) is what damps this controlled roll-past — set it too close to 1 and the ball coasts forever; the usual \(\beta=0.9\) keeps the overshoot small and self-correcting.

RMSProp. Scale each weight's step by the recent size of its gradients, so steep directions take small steps and flat directions take big ones. Keep a running average of squared gradients \(\mathbf{s}\):

\[ \mathbf{s}_{t+1} = \rho\,\mathbf{s}_t + (1-\rho)\,\nabla L(\theta_t)^2, \qquad \theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\mathbf{s}_{t+1}}+\epsilon}\,\nabla L(\theta_t) \]

The \(\epsilon\) (≈ \(10^{-8}\)) just avoids division by zero. RMSProp gives every parameter its own adaptive learning rate.

Adam (Adaptive Moment estimation) — the default optimiser for nearly all of modern deep learning — simply combines momentum and RMSProp. It tracks a running mean of gradients \(\mathbf{m}\) (the momentum idea) and a running mean of squared gradients \(\mathbf{v}\) (the RMSProp idea):

\[ \mathbf{m}_t = \beta_1\mathbf{m}_{t-1} + (1-\beta_1)\nabla L, \qquad \mathbf{v}_t = \beta_2\mathbf{v}_{t-1} + (1-\beta_2)\nabla L^2 \]

After a bias correction (\(\hat{\mathbf{m}}_t = \mathbf{m}_t/(1-\beta_1^t)\) and likewise for \(\hat{\mathbf{v}}_t\), which fixes the cold-start when the averages are near zero), the update is:

\[ \theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{v}}_t}+\epsilon}\,\hat{\mathbf{m}}_t \]

Typical defaults — \(\beta_1=0.9\), \(\beta_2=0.999\), \(\eta=0.001\) — work astonishingly often, which is why Adam is the first optimiser most people reach for.

🔑 The one thing to remember

Momentum smooths the direction of travel (a rolling ball). RMSProp adapts the step size per weight (big steps in flat directions, small in steep). Adam = momentum + RMSProp, and it's the sensible default for almost everything, including transformers in Week 32.

26.4 Regularisation for deep nets

A network with millions of weights can memorise its training data instead of learning the pattern — the overfitting we met in Week 14. Two simple, universal defences:

L2 regularisation (weight decay). Add a penalty on the size of the weights to the loss, discouraging any single weight from growing large:

\[ L_{\text{reg}} = L + \frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_i \theta_i^2 \]

Its gradient adds \(\lambda\theta\) to each weight's gradient, so every update also shrinks the weights a little toward zero — hence the name "weight decay." Smaller weights mean a smoother, simpler function that generalises better. The strength \(\lambda\) is a hyperparameter; it's the same ridge idea from Week 14, now applied to deep nets.

Early stopping. Train while watching the validation loss; the moment it stops falling and begins to rise, stop — that's the point where the network shifts from learning the pattern to memorising the noise. It's free, effective, and the most common regulariser in practice (we previewed it in Week 25).

26.5 SGD vs. momentum, in NumPy

Let's prove momentum helps. We'll minimise a simple stretched quadratic \(L(x,y)=x^2 + 10y^2\) — a narrow valley, steep in \(y\), gentle in \(x\), the exact "ravine" geometry where plain SGD zig-zags. We start from the same point and run both optimisers; momentum reaches the bottom in far fewer steps.

numpy — SGD vs. momentum on a quadratic valley
import numpy as np

# Loss L(x,y) = x^2 + 10 y^2  (a narrow valley: gentle in x, steep in y)
def grad(p):
    x, y = p
    return np.array([2*x, 20*y])     # gradient = [dL/dx, dL/dy]

def loss(p):
    x, y = p
    return x**2 + 10*y**2

start = np.array([9.0, 1.0])   # same starting point for both
eta   = 0.02                   # same learning rate for both

# ---- plain SGD (gradient descent) ----
p = start.copy()
for step in range(60):
    p = p - eta * grad(p)
print("plain SGD  after 60 steps: loss = %.5f" % loss(p))

# ---- SGD with momentum ----
p = start.copy()
v = np.zeros(2)                # velocity, starts at rest
beta = 0.9                     # momentum / friction coefficient
for step in range(60):
    v = beta * v + grad(p)     # accumulate gradient into velocity
    p = p - eta * v            # step with velocity, not raw gradient
print("momentum   after 60 steps: loss = %.5f" % loss(p))

# Momentum's loss is far smaller: it accelerates along x and damps the
# y-axis zig-zag, so it reaches the valley floor in many fewer steps.

Run it and momentum's final loss is dramatically lower than plain SGD's for the same number of steps and the same learning rate — concrete evidence that smoothing the update direction pays off in ravine-shaped landscapes, which is exactly what deep-network loss surfaces look like.

⚠️ Watch out

There is no universal "best" learning rate — it depends on the model, the data, and the optimiser. With plain SGD a rate that works in a gentle direction can diverge in a steep one (that's why our valley is tricky). Adaptive methods like Adam ease this by giving each weight its own effective rate, but you still tune the base \(\eta\). When training won't converge, the learning rate is the first knob to check.
