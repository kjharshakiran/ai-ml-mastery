Probability: Reasoning Under Uncertainty

⏱️ 55 min🎯 The mathematics of doubt📦 Week 4

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to…

  • Treat probability as honest betting — a number from 0 to 1 measuring how strongly you'd back an outcome.
  • Describe a random variable and tell discrete from continuous ones.
  • Read a PMF (for discrete) and a PDF (for continuous) and know why they differ.
  • Compute expectation (the mean) and variance as \(E[(X-\mu)^2]\).
  • Recognise the Normal (Gaussian) distribution, its \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\), and why the Central Limit Theorem makes it appear everywhere.
The Big Question
The real world never hands us certainties — only noisy, partial clues. How do we turn "I'm not sure" into a number a machine can actually compute with?

0. Why are we even doing this?

Why does a course about building intelligent machines spend a week on dice and bell curves? Here's the honest reason.

🎯 The entire point, in one line

The real world is noisy and never certain — blurry photos, typo-ridden text, sensors that wobble. Probability is how we turn "I'm not sure" into a number a machine can compute with. Instead of a model blurting "it's a cat," it says "90% cat, 7% dog, 3% fox." That honesty about uncertainty is what lets a model learn from messy data, know when it's guessing, and improve.

Three concrete reasons it shows up everywhere downstream:

  • Predictions are probabilities. A classifier doesn't output a label, it outputs a distribution over labels (that's what the softmax in Week 24 produces). "How confident?" is a probability.
  • Learning means maximising a probability. Training is often literally "tune the numbers so the correct answers become the most probable" — the maximum likelihood idea you'll meet in Week 16, and the loss functions of nearly every model.
  • Real data is described by distributions. Heights, prices, noise — they cluster into shapes (above all the Gaussian bell curve, section 6). To model data you must speak the language of its spread.
🧒 Explain Like I'm 10

A good weather forecaster never says "it will rain." They say "70% chance of rain," and that number is genuinely useful — you grab an umbrella. A smart machine works the same way: it gives its best guess plus how sure it is. Probability is just the math of saying how sure you are, honestly, as a number.

So as you read about random variables, expectation, and the Gaussian below, keep the purpose in mind: we're teaching the machine to reason with "how likely," not just "yes/no" — because the world only ever gives it clues, never certainties.

1. Probability is honest betting

Linear algebra gave us space, calculus gave us change. Probability gives us the third language machines think in: uncertainty. A probability is simply a number between 0 and 1 that says how strongly you'd bet on something. \(P=0\) means "never," \(P=1\) means "certain," \(P=0.5\) means "I'd take even odds either way."

The rules are just bookkeeping for fair bets: every outcome's probability is between 0 and 1, and the probabilities of all possible outcomes must sum to exactly 1 (something has to happen).

🧒 Explain Like I'm 10

Probability is your fair-bet meter. If a friend says "I'll give you a sweet if it rains tomorrow," how many of your own sweets would you risk to play? If you'd risk a lot, you think rain is likely (high probability). If you'd risk almost nothing, you think it's unlikely (low probability). Probability is just that gut feeling written as a number between 0 (no way) and 1 (for sure). And the honest rule: if you list every possible thing that could happen, your bets have to add up to one whole.

2. Random variables: numbers attached to chance

A random variable (RV) is a number whose value depends on a random outcome. Roll a die → \(X\) is the face you get. Measure a stranger's height → \(H\) is the value in centimetres. The variable is a placeholder; the distribution tells you how its possible values share out the probability.

Two flavours, and the difference matters:

  • Discrete — countable, separate values (a die: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6; the number of emails today).
  • Continuous — any value in a range (height, temperature, a model's predicted score).

3. Discrete RVs: the PMF

For a discrete RV, the probability mass function (PMF) gives the probability of each exact value, \(P(X = x)\). For a fair six-sided die, each face gets a clean \(\tfrac{1}{6}\):

\[ P(X = x) = \tfrac{1}{6}, \quad x \in \{1,2,3,4,5,6\}, \qquad \sum_{x} P(X=x) = 1 \]

"Mass" is a good word: imagine 1 kilogram of probability split into six lumps, one sitting on each face. The lumps must weigh 1 kg in total.

4. Continuous RVs: the PDF

For a continuous RV, asking "what's the probability the height is exactly 170.000…cm?" gives zero — there are infinitely many values, so each single point carries no mass. Instead we use a probability density function (PDF), \(f(x)\), where probability is the area under the curve over a range:

\[ P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx, \qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx = 1 \]

So density isn't probability itself — it's probability per unit width. The total area under any PDF is 1 (using the integration from Week 3). A taller curve in some region just means values there are more likely to turn up.

🔑 PMF vs PDF in one line

Discrete → heights are probabilities (sum to 1). Continuous → areas are probabilities (integral is 1). Bars for the countable, smooth curves for the uncountable.

5. Expectation: the centre of gravity

The expectation (or mean) \(E[X] = \mu\) is the long-run average value — the balance point of the distribution. For a discrete RV it's a probability-weighted sum; for continuous, an integral:

\[ E[X] = \sum_x x\,P(X=x) \qquad \text{or} \qquad E[X] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x\,f(x)\,dx \]

A fair die's expectation is \(\tfrac{1}{6}(1+2+\dots+6) = 3.5\) — not a value you can roll, but the average you'd converge to over many throws.

Variance measures spread: how far values tend to land from the mean. It's the expected squared distance from \(\mu\):

\[ \operatorname{Var}(X) = E\!\big[(X - \mu)^2\big], \qquad \sigma = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)} \]

We square so that overshoots and undershoots don't cancel out, then the square root \(\sigma\) (the standard deviation) brings us back to the original units. Small \(\sigma\) = tightly clustered; large \(\sigma\) = widely scattered.

🎯 Real-World Analogy

Think of an archer's arrows on a target. The mean is the average landing spot — where the cluster's centre sits. The variance is how spread out the arrows are. A sniper has low variance (tight cluster); a beginner has high variance (arrows all over). In ML we want models whose predictions are both well-centred (low bias) and consistent (low variance) — a tension you'll meet head-on in Week 14.

6. The Normal distribution — the bell that's everywhere

One distribution dominates statistics and ML: the Normal (or Gaussian), the famous bell curve. It's fully described by just two numbers — its mean \(\mu\) (where the peak sits) and its standard deviation \(\sigma\) (how wide it is):

\[ f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\, \exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) \]

The curve is symmetric around \(\mu\), and about 68% of the mass sits within one \(\sigma\) of the mean, 95% within two. Why does it show up everywhere — heights, measurement errors, exam scores, noise? Because of the Central Limit Theorem: when you add up lots of small independent random effects, their sum tends toward a Normal distribution regardless of what each individual effect looked like. Heights are sums of many genetic and environmental nudges; errors are sums of many tiny disturbances — so both end up bell-shaped.

Let's sample from a Gaussian and confirm the mean and variance come back close to what we asked for.

numpy — sampling a Gaussian & checking its stats
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(seed=42)   # reproducible randomness

mu, sigma = 170.0, 8.0                 # e.g. adult heights: mean 170 cm, sd 8 cm
samples = rng.normal(mu, sigma, size=100_000)   # draw 100k values from N(mu, sigma^2)

# Expectation E[X] estimated by the sample mean
print(f"sample mean     : {samples.mean():.3f}")     # -> ~170.0  (matches mu)

# Variance E[(X - mu)^2] estimated from the data; sd is its square root
print(f"sample variance : {samples.var():.3f}")      # -> ~64.0   (sigma^2 = 8^2)
print(f"sample std dev  : {samples.std():.3f}")      # -> ~8.0    (matches sigma)

# The "68% within one sigma" rule of thumb, checked empirically
within_1sd = np.mean(np.abs(samples - mu) <= sigma)
print(f"fraction within 1 sigma: {within_1sd:.3f}")  # -> ~0.683

# Evaluate the Gaussian PDF formula by hand at the mean (its peak height)
peak = 1 / (sigma * np.sqrt(2 * np.pi))
print(f"PDF height at x=mu: {peak:.5f}")             # -> 0.04988

The sample mean and variance land right on the values we requested, and almost exactly 68.3% of draws fall within one \(\sigma\) — the bell curve behaving exactly as the formula promises.

✅ Check Yourself

For a continuous random variable, the probability that \(X\) equals one exact value (say exactly 170.000…) is…
A PDF gives probability as area under the curve over an interval. A single point has zero width, hence zero area, hence zero probability. We only get nonzero probabilities for ranges: \(P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx\).
Variance is defined as \(E[(X-\mu)^2]\). Why square the distance from the mean?
Without squaring, positive and negative deviations would cancel to zero. Squaring makes every deviation positive and penalises large ones extra. Taking the square root afterward gives \(\sigma\), which is back in the original units.
Why does the Gaussian (Normal) distribution show up so often in nature and data?
The Central Limit Theorem says that adding up many independent random contributions yields an approximately Normal result regardless of each contribution's own shape. Heights, errors, and noise are all such sums — so they end up bell-shaped.
