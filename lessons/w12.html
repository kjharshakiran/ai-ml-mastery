🧮 Part 4 · Classical Machine Learning

Paradigms of ML II: Self-supervised, Online, Meta & RL

⏱️ 45 min🎯 The paradigms that power modern AI📦 Week 12

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to…

  • Use a few labels well with semi-supervised and active learning.
  • Explain self-supervised learning — and why "predict the next word" is the engine behind every LLM.
  • Describe online learning: training on a stream, one example at a time.
  • Grasp meta-learning — "learning to learn" — and why it enables few-shot learning.
  • Preview reinforcement learning: learning to act from rewards.
The Big Question
Labelling data is slow and expensive — yet ChatGPT learned from trillions of words that nobody hand-labelled. What's the trick that lets a model teach itself?

1. Between the two worlds: semi-supervised & active learning

Last week's tree had two big branches — fully labelled (supervised) and fully unlabelled (unsupervised). Real life usually sits in the middle: a few labelled examples and a mountain of unlabelled ones, because labelling costs human time. Two paradigms exploit this.

Semi-supervised learning uses the small labelled set to anchor the model, then leans on the huge unlabelled set to sharpen the decision boundary. The key assumption: points that are close together (or sit on the same dense "blob" of data) probably share a label. A handful of labels can propagate through the structure of the unlabelled cloud.

Active learning is cleverer still: the model itself chooses which examples to ask a human to label. Rather than labelling 10,000 random images, it requests labels only for the examples it's most unsure about — the ones near its decision boundary — getting the most learning per expensive label.

🙋 Real-World Analogy

Active learning is the smart student who doesn't re-read the whole textbook — they ask the teacher only about the exact problems that confuse them. Each question is targeted at maximum confusion, so each answer teaches the most. That's how to spend a limited "labelling budget" wisely.

2. Self-supervised learning: the data labels itself

Here is the idea that changed AI. Self-supervised learning creates labels out of the data itself — no human annotators needed. You take unlabelled data, hide part of it, and train the model to predict the hidden part from the rest. The "label" is just the piece you covered up, so it's free and infinite.

🔑 The trick that built the LLM era

Give a model billions of sentences and one task: predict the next word. "The cat sat on the ___" → mat. The correct answer was already in the text — you just hid it. Nobody labelled anything, yet to predict the next word well the model must learn grammar, facts, reasoning, and style. This single self-supervised objective is how GPT-style models are pre-trained.

Formally it's still supervised machinery — there's an input, a target, and a loss — but the target is manufactured from the input. For next-word prediction we maximise the probability the model assigns to each true next token \(x_t\) given everything before it:

\[ \mathcal{L} = -\sum_{t} \log P_\theta\bigl(x_t \mid x_1, x_2, \dots, x_{t-1}\bigr) \]

Because the supervision comes for free, you can train on the entire internet. That's why self-supervision unlocked models of a scale that hand-labelling never could.

🧒 Explain Like I'm 10

It's like a fill-in-the-blank game you can play forever without a teacher. Cover the last word of any sentence and guess it. If you're wrong, peek and fix your guess. Play this billions of times with every book and website ever written, and you'd end up knowing an astonishing amount about the world — just from guessing missing words. That's how ChatGPT learned.

3. Online learning: training on a moving river

Everything so far assumed a fixed dataset sitting in memory ("batch" learning). But data often arrives as a stream — stock ticks, clicks, sensor readings, fraud attempts — and it never stops. Online learning updates the model one example (or one small mini-batch) at a time, then throws that example away.

This matters for two reasons: (1) datasets too big to fit in memory can still be learned from in a single pass, and (2) the world drifts — yesterday's spam looks nothing like today's — so a model that keeps updating stays fresh. The update rule is the same gradient step you'll meet in Week 26, just applied per-example instead of over the whole batch.

🌊 Real-World Analogy

Batch learning is studying from a finished textbook. Online learning is being a news editor: stories pour in all day and you update your understanding of "what's happening" continuously, never waiting for the year to end. You adapt as the river flows.

4. Meta-learning: learning to learn

Ordinary ML learns one task. Meta-learning learns how to learn across many tasks, so that facing a brand-new task it can master it from just a handful of examples. The model isn't memorising answers — it's acquiring a learning strategy: good starting weights, or a good way to adapt quickly.

This is the engine behind few-shot learning: show the system 3 examples of a category it's never seen and it generalises. It works because, having trained on thousands of different small tasks, it has learned the general shape of "how tasks like this tend to go," so a few examples are enough to pin down the specifics.

🧒 Explain Like I'm 10

A kid who has learned ten board games can pick up an eleventh almost instantly — not because they memorised the new rules in advance, but because they got good at learning games in general. Meta-learning is teaching a model to be that quick learner.

5. Reinforcement learning: learning by trial, reward, and error

The last great branch of the tree is utterly different. There's no fixed dataset and no answer key. Instead an agent acts in an environment, and the only feedback is a reward — a number saying how good the outcome was. The agent must figure out, through trial and error, which actions lead to the most reward over time.

This is how AlphaGo learned to beat world champions and how robots learn to walk. It's also a final-stage ingredient in modern chatbots: after self-supervised pre-training, models are polished with RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback) so their answers are helpful and safe. We'll do MDPs, value functions, and policies properly in Week 40.

6. Self-supervision in a few lines

The whole magic of section 2 is that labels are manufactured from raw text. Here's the core idea in plain NumPy: slide a window over a sentence and turn it into (context → next word) training pairs — free labels, no human in sight.

numpy — building self-supervised labels
import numpy as np

text = "the cat sat on the mat".split()      # raw, unlabelled data
ctx = 2                                       # use 2 previous words as context

# Manufacture (context, target) pairs by hiding the next word -- this is the "label"
pairs = [(text[i-ctx:i], text[i]) for i in range(ctx, len(text))]
for context, target in pairs:
    print(context, "->", target)
# ['the', 'cat'] -> sat   |   ['cat', 'sat'] -> on   |  ... etc.

# Count how often each context predicts each next word: a tiny "language model"
vocab = sorted(set(text))
idx = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(vocab)}
counts = np.zeros((len(vocab), len(vocab)))   # counts[prev, next]
for i in range(1, len(text)):
    counts[idx[text[i-1]], idx[text[i]]] += 1  # the target came free from the data
print("after 'the', model predicts:", vocab[int(counts[idx['the']].argmax())])  # -> 'cat'

No annotator labelled anything — every target was simply the word we hid. Scale this exact idea from one sentence to trillions of words and from counts to a transformer, and you have the pre-training recipe behind every modern LLM.

✅ Check Yourself

Why is "predict the next word" called self-supervised rather than supervised?
The target is manufactured by hiding part of the input. The "answer" was already in the text, so supervision is free and effectively infinite — which is exactly why LLMs can train on the whole internet.
A fraud-detection system must update continuously as new transactions stream in, never stopping to retrain from scratch. This is…
Learning one example at a time from a never-ending stream, adapting as the data drifts, is the definition of online learning.
In reinforcement learning, what feedback does the agent learn from?
RL has no answer key. The agent takes actions and receives rewards, then learns a policy that maximises long-run reward through trial and error.
