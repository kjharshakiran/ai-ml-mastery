The Big Question
GANs play a clever cat-and-mouse game to fake data. But what if, instead of an adversary, we taught a model to do something far simpler — undo noise — and discovered that this single skill produces the most stunning images, audio and molecules of all?

37.1 Generative AI is everywhere now

Across Weeks 32–36 you built the machinery of generation: transformers that produce text, and GANs that produce images. Step back and the applications are sweeping:

  • Art & media. Type a sentence and get an illustration, a logo, a concept render, a song, a short video. Tools like Midjourney and Stable Diffusion turned image creation into a conversation.
  • Code. Models like GitHub Copilot generate functions from comments — an LLM trained on billions of lines of source, completing yours in context.
  • Drug discovery. Generative models propose novel molecules with desired properties, and models like AlphaFold predict protein structure — searching a chemical space far too vast to enumerate by hand.
  • Synthetic data. When real data is scarce, private, or imbalanced (rare medical conditions, fraud), a generative model can manufacture realistic training examples — privacy-preserving fuel for other models.

Underneath this variety sit just a handful of model families. Let's organise them.

🧒 Explain Like I'm 10

Imagine you have a magic photo of a sandcastle, and slowly you sprinkle sand over it until it's just a flat pile — totally messy. Now imagine learning, grain by grain, how to un-sprinkle: take a flat pile of sand and carefully scrape it back into a perfect castle. A diffusion model learns exactly that un-sprinkling trick. Start with pure random "static," and it patiently cleans the static into a picture of whatever you asked for.

37.2 Generating text: LLMs, revisited

For text, the dominant generative model is the Large Language Model from Week 34. An LLM is a transformer trained to predict the next token given everything before it:

\[ p(\text{sentence}) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} p\big(x_t \mid x_1, x_2, \dots, x_{t-1}\big). \]

Generation is then just sampling one token at a time and feeding it back in — autoregression. Because the model has read a huge slice of the internet, those conditional probabilities encode grammar, facts, reasoning patterns and style. This is a fundamentally different generative recipe from images: text is produced left-to-right, one symbol at a time, whereas the image models below produce a whole canvas all at once and refine it. Same goal — sample from \(p(\text{data})\) — different mechanics suited to the medium.

37.3 Generating images: enter diffusion models

For images, the state of the art shifted from GANs to diffusion models. The idea is gorgeous in its simplicity and rests on a fact you met in Week 4: Gaussian noise.

Forward process (fixed, no learning). Take a real image and add a little Gaussian noise. Repeat for many steps \(t=1\dots T\). After enough steps the image is indistinguishable from pure static \(\mathcal{N}(0,I)\). Each step is

\[ \mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,\mathbf{x}_{t-1} + \sqrt{\beta_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I), \]

where the small \(\beta_t\) is the amount of noise added at step \(t\). This direction needs no training — it's just destruction by Gaussian noise.

Reverse process (this is what we learn). Train a neural network — typically a U-Net (Week 29) — to look at a noisy \(\mathbf{x}_t\) and predict the noise that was added, so we can subtract it and step back toward \(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\). Do this \(T\) times starting from pure static and a clean image emerges. The training loss is simply: predict the noise.

\[ \mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_0,\,t,\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\Big[\big\lVert \boldsymbol{\epsilon} - \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)\big\rVert^2\Big]. \]

🗿 Real-World Analogy

Michelangelo said he just "removed everything that wasn't the statue." Diffusion is that, in reverse-fog. The forward process is fog rolling in until a sculpture vanishes into grey. The model studies thousands of "foggy → clear" snapshots until it can stand in front of a wall of pure fog and, layer by layer, reveal a statue that was never actually there — guided by your prompt about what statue to find.

37.4 Diffusion vs GANs vs VAEs

All three are generative — they learn to sample new data — but they get there differently:

  • GANs (Weeks 35–36). One generator, one adversary. Fast to sample (one forward pass) and sharp, but training is a fragile balancing act prone to mode collapse.
  • Diffusion. No adversary — just a stable regression loss (predict the noise). Slower to sample (many denoising steps) but remarkably stable to train, with excellent quality and diversity. This stability is why it overtook GANs for text-to-image.
  • VAEs (Variational Autoencoders). An encoder squeezes data into a probabilistic latent code and a decoder reconstructs it, trained to make that latent space smooth and samplable. VAEs are easy to train and give a clean latent space, but their samples tend to look blurrier than GAN or diffusion outputs. (Modern systems like Stable Diffusion actually combine ideas — running diffusion inside a VAE's compressed latent space for speed.)
🔑 The one thing to remember

A diffusion model learns one humble skill — predict and remove the noise — and by applying it many times from pure static, it generates. No adversary, no minimax: just a stable denoising regression. That stability, not raw cleverness, is what made it the engine of Stable Diffusion, DALL·E and Midjourney.

37.5 Code: simulating the forward process

You don't need a GPU to feel how diffusion's forward process works — it's literally "add Gaussian noise, repeatedly." Here we corrupt a clean 1-D signal (a sine wave) step by step and watch it dissolve into static. The reverse direction is what a trained network would learn; the forward direction is free.

numpy — forward diffusion
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# A clean "signal" (our stand-in for an image): one period of a sine wave,
# sampled at 256 points so the standard deviation is a stable estimate.
x0 = np.sin(np.linspace(0, 2 * np.pi, 256))
print("clean x0 std :", round(float(x0.std()), 2))   # ~0.71

T = 50                                   # number of diffusion steps
betas = np.linspace(1e-3, 0.2, T)        # noise schedule: a little noise -> more over time

x = x0.copy()
for t in range(T):
    b = betas[t]
    noise = rng.standard_normal(x.shape)               # fresh Gaussian noise (Week 4!)
    # one forward step: shrink the signal slightly, then add a dab of noise
    x = np.sqrt(1 - b) * x + np.sqrt(b) * noise
    if t in (0, 9, 24, 49):
        print(f"step {t+1:2d} | std={x.std():.2f}")   # std climbs toward 1.0 as structure dissolves

# As t grows, the signal washes out and x looks like pure standard-normal static.
# A diffusion MODEL would learn the reverse: predict 'noise' at each step and subtract it,
# walking from static all the way back to a clean, NEW signal.
print("final std ~", round(float(x.std()), 2))   # close to 1.0  -> pure N(0, I) noise

Run it and you'll watch the sine wave's structure fade as the standard deviation creeps toward 1.0 — the signature of pure \(\mathcal{N}(0,I)\) noise. Training a model to invert each of those 50 steps is the whole game of image generation.

⚠️ Watch out

The forward process is fixed and free — there is nothing to learn there. A common confusion is thinking the model "learns to add noise." It doesn't: it learns only the reverse, predicting the noise so it can remove it. The forward chain just generates the (noisy image → noise) training pairs.

✅ Check Yourself

In a diffusion model, what does the neural network actually learn?
The forward (noising) process is fixed and needs no learning. The network learns only the reverse: given a noisy image, predict the noise so it can be subtracted, walking from static back to a clean image.
A key reason diffusion models overtook GANs for text-to-image was that they…
Diffusion trades sampling speed (many steps) for training stability: there's no minimax game, just a noise-prediction regression. That stability gives both high quality and high diversity, which is why it won out for text-to-image.
Which statement about the three model families is correct?
GANs: fast, sharp, fragile (mode collapse). Diffusion: stable, high-quality, slower to sample. VAEs: easy to train with a clean latent space but blurrier samples. Modern systems often combine them.
