The Big Question
Before you fit a single model, how do you find out whether your data is trustworthy — full of typos, missing chunks, or hiding a beautiful pattern — using just a handful of numbers and plots?

8.1 What EDA is, and why it comes first

Exploratory Data Analysis is the detective phase: you summarise, visualise, and question your data before modelling. Coined by statistician John Tukey, EDA's whole philosophy is "let the data speak." Skip it and you'll train a flawless model on garbage — and trust its confident, wrong answers.

🧒 Explain Like I'm 10

EDA is a doctor's check-up for your dataset. Before any treatment (the model), the doctor takes the basics: temperature, height, weight (the averages), checks for anything missing or weird (a broken arm = an outlier), and asks whether things go together ("does eating more relate to weight?"). Only after the check-up does treatment begin. Skipping the check-up is how you prescribe the wrong medicine.

8.2 Central tendency & dispersion

Two questions describe any column of numbers: where is the middle? (central tendency) and how spread out is it? (dispersion). These are exactly the statistics from Week 4, now computed on real data.

Central tendency:

  • Mean \( \bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i x_i \) — the average. Sensitive to outliers.
  • Median — the middle value when sorted. Robust: one billionaire doesn't move it.
  • Mode — the most frequent value. The only one that works for categories ("most common city").

Dispersion:

  • Variance \( \sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})^2 \) — the average squared distance from the mean.
  • Standard deviation \( \sigma = \sqrt{\sigma^2} \) — spread in the data's own units. The Gaussian's width from Week 4.
  • IQR (interquartile range) \( = Q_3 - Q_1 \) — the spread of the middle 50%. Robust to outliers, and the basis of outlier detection.
🔑 Mean or median?

When a distribution is roughly symmetric, mean ≈ median — use the mean. When it's skewed or has outliers (incomes, house prices, response times), the median tells the honest story. "Average salary" reported as a mean is how a room with one billionaire looks rich on paper.

8.3 Data cleaning: missing values, outliers, types

Real data is messy. Three problems show up every single time:

  • Missing values (NaN, blanks). Options: drop the rows, or impute — fill with the mean/median (numeric) or mode (categorical). Dropping loses data; imputing risks bias. Choose deliberately.
  • Outliers — values far from the rest. A common rule: anything below \( Q_1 - 1.5\,\text{IQR} \) or above \( Q_3 + 1.5\,\text{IQR} \) is suspect. Some are errors (age = 200); some are real and important (fraud). Investigate before deleting.
  • Wrong types — a price stored as text "1,650", a date as a string. Convert before computing, or the math silently breaks.
⚠️ An outlier is a question, not a verdict

Never auto-delete outliers. The most valuable rows in fraud detection, anomaly monitoring, and medical data are the outliers. Flag them, understand them, then decide. Deleting "weird" rows is how you delete exactly the signal you were hired to find.

8.4 & 8.5 Relationships: correlation & distributions

EDA gets interesting when you ask how features move together. The Pearson correlation \( r \) measures linear association, from −1 to +1:

\[ r_{xy} = \frac{\operatorname{cov}(x,y)}{\sigma_x\,\sigma_y} = \frac{\sum_i (x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y})}{\sqrt{\sum_i (x_i-\bar{x})^2}\,\sqrt{\sum_i (y_i-\bar{y})^2}} \]

  • \( r \approx +1 \): move together (more rooms → higher price).
  • \( r \approx 0 \): no linear relationship (it could still be a curve!).
  • \( r \approx -1 \): move oppositely (more age of car → lower price).

Notice this is the dot product from Week 2 in disguise — correlation is the cosine similarity of two mean-centred feature columns. Geometry and statistics meet here.

🌡️ Real-World Analogy

Correlation is like noticing that ice-cream sales and sunburns rise together. They're correlated (\(r\) high) — but ice cream doesn't cause sunburn; the sun causes both. Correlation is not causation. EDA reveals what moves together; it takes careful experiment design to prove what causes what.

8.6 EDA in pandas — the practical toolkit

pandas (Week 7) makes all of this a few lines. A DataFrame is a labelled table — the query result from Week 5, now in Python with superpowers.

python — EDA with pandas
import pandas as pd
import numpy as np

# A small, messy customer dataset (note the NaN and an outlier)
df = pd.DataFrame({
    "city":  ["Mumbai", "Delhi", "Mumbai", "Delhi", "Mumbai"],
    "age":   [31, 45, np.nan, 38, 29],          # one missing value
    "spend": [1650, 300, 2400, 280, 99000],     # 99000 is an outlier
})

# 1. The one-line summary: count, mean, std, min, quartiles, max
print(df.describe())          # central tendency + dispersion at a glance
# Notice mean spend is dragged way up by the 99000 outlier...
print("median spend:", df["spend"].median())   # ...median stays honest

# 2. Find missing values, then impute age with the median
print(df.isna().sum())                         # how many NaNs per column
df["age"] = df["age"].fillna(df["age"].median())   # robust fill

# 3. Flag outliers with the 1.5 * IQR rule
q1, q3 = df["spend"].quantile([0.25, 0.75])
iqr = q3 - q1
high_cut = q3 + 1.5 * iqr
df["is_outlier"] = df["spend"] > high_cut
print(df[df["is_outlier"]])    # investigate, don't auto-delete!

# 4. Mode for the categorical column
print("most common city:", df["city"].mode()[0])   # -> Mumbai

# 5. Correlation between numeric features (linear relationships)
print(df[["age", "spend"]].corr())   # r near 0 here = no linear link

That single df.describe() call computes count, mean, std, min, the quartiles (for IQR), and max — every number from sections 8.2–8.3 at once. It's the same COUNT/AVG/MIN/MAX aggregation you wrote in SQL back in Week 5, now one method on a DataFrame.

✅ Check Yourself

Your "spend" column has one customer at ₹99,000 while everyone else is under ₹3,000. Which summary best describes the typical customer?
The mean gets pulled toward the ₹99,000 outlier, overstating "typical." The median sits at the middle of the sorted values and barely moves — the robust choice for skewed data.
A feature shows correlation r = 0.95 with the target. What is the safest conclusion?
High \(r\) means a strong linear relationship, useful for modelling — but correlation is not causation (ice cream and sunburns). Also, \(r\) only captures linear structure; curves can have \(r \approx 0\) yet be tightly related.
In pandas, what does df.describe() give you in one call?
describe() returns count, mean, std, min, 25/50/75% quartiles, and max — the central-tendency and dispersion summary of sections 8.2–8.3, and the same aggregates you computed with SQL in Week 5.
