RNNs II: Seq2Seq & Word Embeddings

⏱️ 60 min🎯 Turning words into geometry, and sequences into sequences📦 Week 31

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to…

  • Chain two RNNs into an encoder–decoder (seq2seq) for tasks like translation.
  • Spot the information bottleneck of a single context vector — and why it motivates attention.
  • Explain word embeddings (word2vec, GloVe): a dense vector per word, with similar words nearby.
  • Understand the famous analogy \(\text{king}-\text{man}+\text{woman}\approx\text{queen}\) as vector arithmetic.
  • Evaluate sequence models with perplexity and BLEU.
The Big Question
Back in Week 1 we promised that "words become vectors." But which vectors? If we could place every word in space so that meaning becomes distance — so "Paris is to France as Tokyo is to Japan" turns into plain arithmetic — what could a machine then do with language?

31.1 Sequential processing, recap and reach

Last week (Week 30) we gave a network memory: an RNN reads a sequence one step at a time, carrying a hidden state forward, and LSTM/GRU gates let that memory survive across long stretches. We saw the shapes — many-to-one (classify a review), one-to-many (caption an image). This lesson tackles the hardest and most useful shape: many-to-many of different lengths, where a sequence comes in and a differently sized sequence comes out. The flagship example is machine translation: "the cat sat" (3 English words) → "le chat s'est assis" (4 French words). You can't tag word-for-word; the whole sentence must be understood first.

31.2 Seq2Seq: encode, then decode

The breakthrough idea (Sutskever et al., 2014) is to use two RNNs. The encoder reads the entire source sentence and squeezes everything it understood into a single fixed-length vector — the context vector (it's just the encoder's final hidden state). Think of it as a numerical summary of the sentence's meaning. The decoder is a second RNN that takes that context vector as its starting memory and generates the target sentence one word at a time, feeding each word it produces back in as the next input until it emits an end-of-sentence token.

⚠️ The bottleneck

Cramming an entire 40-word sentence into one fixed-size vector is like summarising a novel on a single sticky note — fine for a short story, hopeless for War and Peace. The decoder, gazing at one frozen vector, struggles to recall details from the start of a long source. This information bottleneck is the precise weakness that attention (Week 32) was invented to remove: let the decoder look back at all the encoder states, not just the last one.

📞 Real-World Analogy

Seq2Seq is like a translator listening to a whole sentence, then closing their eyes and writing down one tight summary on an index card — and a second person who can only see that card must reproduce the sentence in French. For "the cat sat" the card is plenty. For a winding 40-word legal clause, everything important has to fit on the same small card, and details from the opening get squeezed out. That cramped card is the bottleneck.

31.3 Word embeddings: meaning becomes geometry

We've been glossing over a basic question: what is a "word" to a network? The naive answer is one-hot encoding — a vector as long as the vocabulary (say 50,000), all zeros except a single 1. But this is terrible: it's enormous, wasteful, and worst of all every pair of words is equally distant. "cat" and "kitten" are as unrelated as "cat" and "helicopter." There's no notion of meaning.

Word embeddings fix this. Each word gets a short dense vector — maybe 300 real numbers — and these vectors are learned so that words used in similar contexts land near each other in space. This is the realisation of Week 1's promise that "words become vectors," and it leans entirely on Week 2's geometry: closeness is measured by cosine similarity, the normalised dot product. The guiding principle (the "distributional hypothesis") is delightfully simple: you shall know a word by the company it keeps. Words appearing in similar surroundings ("the ___ purred", "I fed the ___") get similar vectors.

The two classic methods learn exactly this. word2vec (Mikolov, 2013) trains a tiny network to predict a word from its neighbours (or vice-versa); its hidden weights become the embeddings. GloVe (Stanford) instead factorises a giant word-co-occurrence count matrix. Both yield the same magic.

And the magic is that directions in this space carry meaning. The vector offset from "man" to "woman" turns out to be roughly the same as from "king" to "queen" — a "gender" direction. So semantic relationships become arithmetic:

\[ \text{vec}(\text{king}) - \text{vec}(\text{man}) + \text{vec}(\text{woman}) \;\approx\; \text{vec}(\text{queen}) \]

With the toy vectors below this is literal arithmetic: \(\text{king}-\text{man}+\text{woman} = [0.9,0.9,0.1] - [0.1,0.9,0.1] + [0.1,0.1,0.9] = [0.9,0.1,0.9]\), which is exactly queen's vector — so their cosine similarity is \(1.0\), a perfect match.

We measure "nearby" with cosine similarity from Week 2:

\[ \cos(\theta) \;=\; \frac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{\lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert} \]

🧒 Explain Like I'm 10

Imagine a magic map of all words, where words that mean similar things sit close together — "happy" near "glad," "dog" near "puppy." On this map, certain directions have meaning. Walking the "boy → girl" direction from "king" drops you right at "queen." So the computer can do word math: start at king, walk the girl direction, arrive at queen. Words turned into places, and meaning turned into walking directions.

numpy — embedding lookup + cosine similarity + word math
import numpy as np

# A toy 3-dim embedding table (real ones are ~300-dim, learned from text).
# Axes loosely mean [royalty, male-ness, female-ness] for illustration.
embeddings = {
    "king":  np.array([0.9, 0.9, 0.1]),
    "queen": np.array([0.9, 0.1, 0.9]),
    "man":   np.array([0.1, 0.9, 0.1]),
    "woman": np.array([0.1, 0.1, 0.9]),
}

def cosine(a, b):
    # Week 2's cosine similarity: dot product, normalised by lengths.
    return np.dot(a, b) / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b))

# 1) Embedding lookup is just dictionary access (a table row per word).
print("cos(king, queen) =", round(cosine(embeddings["king"],
                                          embeddings["queen"]), 3))
print("cos(king, woman) =", round(cosine(embeddings["king"],
                                          embeddings["woman"]), 3))

# 2) The famous analogy as vector arithmetic:
#    king - man + woman should land closest to "queen".
result = embeddings["king"] - embeddings["man"] + embeddings["woman"]

# 3) Find the nearest known word to that result vector.
best_word, best_score = None, -1.0
for word, vec in embeddings.items():
    if word in ("king", "man", "woman"):     # exclude the inputs themselves
        continue
    s = cosine(result, vec)
    if s > best_score:
        best_word, best_score = word, s

print("king - man + woman is closest to:", best_word)   # -> queen

31.4 Evaluation: perplexity and BLEU

How do we score these models? For a language model (which predicts the next word) we use perplexity — roughly, "how surprised is the model by the real next word, on average?" It's the exponential of the average cross-entropy loss (Week 25); lower is better, and a perplexity of \(k\) means the model is as confused as if it were choosing uniformly among \(k\) words. Concretely, a model that assigns probability \(\tfrac{1}{10}\) on average to the true next word has perplexity \(10\). For translation, where many outputs are valid, we use BLEU, which checks how many short word-sequences (n-grams) in the machine's output also appear in human reference translations. BLEU ranges 0–1 (often shown ×100); higher means closer to human translations. Neither metric is perfect — BLEU misses meaning-preserving paraphrases — but they let us compare models objectively.

🔑 The one thing to remember

A word embedding is a learned dense vector per word where distance means similarity and directions mean relationships. It turns language into geometry so the dot product can measure meaning — and it's the input layer of essentially every modern NLP model, including the transformers and LLMs coming next.

🔗 Connect the Dots

This lesson realises Week 1's promise that "everything becomes a vector" and runs entirely on Week 2's cosine similarity to measure word meaning. The seq2seq architecture chains the RNNs from Week 30. Its single-context-vector bottleneck is the exact problem that attention & transformers (Week 32) solve — letting the decoder peek at every encoder state. And these embeddings become the literal first layer of Project 4 (Mini-GPT), where you'll build a transformer from embeddings up.

✅ Check Yourself

In a basic seq2seq model, what is the "context vector"?
The encoder compresses the entire input into this one vector; the decoder generates from it. Forcing all meaning through one fixed vector is the bottleneck that attention later fixes.
Why are word embeddings better than one-hot vectors?
One-hot vectors are huge and treat all words as equally unrelated. Embeddings are short, learned, and geometric — "cat" sits near "kitten," and directions capture relationships like gender or tense.
\(\text{king}-\text{man}+\text{woman}\approx\text{queen}\) works because…
The "male→female" offset is roughly the same vector everywhere, so subtracting "man" and adding "woman" shifts "king" along that direction and lands near "queen."
