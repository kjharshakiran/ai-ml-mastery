Linear Algebra: Vectors, Matrices & Eigen-everything

⏱️ 60 min🎯 The language of data📦 Week 2

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to…

  • See a vector two ways: as an arrow in space and as a list of data.
  • Add, scale, and measure vectors (norms) and find the angle between them (dot product).
  • Read a matrix as a machine that transforms space.
  • Understand eigenvectors/eigenvalues, eigendecomposition, and SVD — and why they power PCA, embeddings, and recommendation systems.
  • Do all of it in NumPy.
The Big Question
If every photo, word, and customer is just a list of numbers, what can we do with those lists to make patterns appear?

0. Why are we even doing this?

Before a single formula, let's answer the question every honest learner asks: why matrices, why these operations, what is the point? Most courses skip this and you end up pushing symbols around without knowing why. Here's the whole motivation in one sentence:

🎯 The entire point, in one line

A matrix is just how you do the same weighted-sum to a whole pile of data at once. Almost everything a model computes is: take some input numbers, mix them into new numbers by scaling and adding. "Scale and add," done in bulk, is matrix multiplication. That's it — the rest of this lesson is detail.

Let's watch matrices appear on their own from a problem you already understand: predicting a house price. One house is a vector of features, and your model has learned how much each feature is worth (the weights):

\[ \mathbf{x} = \underbrace{[\,3,\;2,\;1500\,]}_{\text{beds, baths, area}}, \qquad \mathbf{w} = \underbrace{[\,50,\;30,\;0.2\,]}_{\text{value of each, in }\$k} \]

The prediction is just "scale each feature by its weight and add them up":

\[ \text{price} = 3\cdot 50 + 2\cdot 30 + 1500\cdot 0.2 = 150 + 60 + 300 = 510 \]

That weighted sum is a dot product (section 3). So the dot product isn't an abstract exercise — it's literally "make one prediction." Now watch a matrix build itself in two steps:

  • "What about my other 1000 houses?" You don't want to write that dot product 1000 times. Stack the houses as rows of a grid \(X\) (1000 rows × 3 features) and write one thing: \(X\mathbf{w}\) → all 1000 prices at once. That grid is a matrix — it's literally your spreadsheet of data.
  • "I want to predict price and rent and tax." Now you have three weight-vectors. Stand them up as three columns of a matrix \(W\). Then \(XW\) → 1000 houses × 3 predictions each. Nothing new happened — it's the same scale-and-add, just bookkept so you do many items × many outputs in one move.
🧒 Explain Like I'm 10

Imagine a juice recipe: "2 oranges + 1 apple + 3 grapes." That's a weighted sum — a dot product. Now imagine a whole factory making 1000 juices from 1000 fruit-baskets using that same recipe. Instead of doing them one by one, you feed all the baskets through one machine and get all 1000 juices out the other end. The machine is the matrix. It does the same simple mixing to a huge pile of stuff in one go.

So, why these operations? They're the only LEGO bricks you need, and there are really just two: scale a vector and add vectors. Together they make a linear combination, and everything a matrix does is linear combinations of your inputs. The dot product is one weighted sum (predict / compare / "how similar"). Matrix multiply is many dot products (transform the whole dataset at once). Eigenvectors and SVD (sections 6–8) then let you look at a transformation and find its skeleton — so you can simplify and compress it.

🔗 Why this is the whole course in miniature

Keep this mental model: data are vectors (points in space); a matrix is a verb that moves all those points at once; and "learning" = finding the right matrix of numbers so that after the move, the useful pattern lines up. A neural-network layer is literally output = activation(W·x + b) — a matrix multiply (Week 24). Deep learning is mostly stacking matrix multiplies. And in a transformer, "attention" is \(QK^\top\) — a grid of dot products asking "how relevant is every word to every other word?" all at once (Week 32). Same idea you just saw with houses, getting richer all the way up to ChatGPT.

1. A vector is two things at once

In Lesson 1 we said "everything becomes a vector." Now let's actually meet one. A vector like \(\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\) has two equally true interpretations, and switching between them on demand is the single most useful skill in this course:

  1. An arrow in space — start at the origin, walk 3 right and 2 up. This is the geometric view. It makes "distance," "direction," and "angle" meaningful.
  2. A list of features — e.g. a house = [bedrooms, bathrooms] = [3, 2]. This is the data view.
🗺️ Real-World Analogy

A vector is like a treasure-map instruction: "3 paces east, 2 paces north." The instruction (the numbers) and the actual spot on the ground (the arrow) are the same information in two costumes. Data scientists wear the "list of numbers" costume; geometry wears the "arrow" costume. Pros switch costumes mid-sentence.

🧒 Explain Like I'm 10

A vector is just a list of numbers in a fixed order — like a recipe: [2 eggs, 1 cup flour, 3 spoons sugar]. Change the order and it's a different recipe. Computers love these lists because they can do maths on them. When we say "a cat photo is a vector," we just mean we wrote down a long list of numbers (the brightness of each dot) that stands for the picture.

2. The two operations that build everything: add & scale

You only ever do two fundamental things to vectors, and every fancy algorithm later is built from these:

Addition (tip-to-tail): \(\begin{bmatrix}3\\2\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}4\\4\end{bmatrix}\). Geometrically, walk the first arrow, then walk the second from where you landed.

Scaling (stretch/shrink): \(2 \cdot \begin{bmatrix}3\\2\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}6\\4\end{bmatrix}\). Same direction, twice as long. A negative scalar flips it around.

Combine these — "add up scaled copies of some vectors" — and you get a linear combination, the most important phrase in linear algebra. A neural network layer? A giant pile of linear combinations. Hold that thought until Week 24.

3. The dot product: how similar are two vectors?

The dot product takes two vectors and returns a single number that measures how much they point the same way:

\[ \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = \sum_i a_i b_i = a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + \dots = \lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert\cos\theta \]

  • Big positive → they point the same way (similar).
  • Zero → they're perpendicular (unrelated). This is huge: "uncorrelated" = "orthogonal."
  • Negative → they point opposite ways.
🔑 Why you'll never forget this

"How similar are two things?" is the question in machine learning — similar customers, similar documents, similar words. The dot product (and its cousin, cosine similarity) is the answer, and it shows up literally everywhere: in k-NN (Week 15), in SVMs (Week 18), and as the beating heart of attention in transformers (Week 32), where the model asks "how relevant is each word to this one?" with a dot product.

4. The norm: how long is a vector?

The norm \(\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert\) is the vector's length — straight from Pythagoras:

\[ \lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert_2 = \sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2 + \dots + v_n^2} = \sqrt{\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v}} \]

The distance between two data points \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\) is just \(\lVert\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}\rVert\). That one formula is how a model decides "these two houses are similar" or "this email looks like spam I've seen before."

numpy
import numpy as np

a = np.array([3, 2])
b = np.array([1, 2])

a + b              # vector addition  -> array([4, 4])
2 * a              # scaling          -> array([6, 4])
np.dot(a, b)       # dot product      -> 3*1 + 2*2 = 7
np.linalg.norm(a)  # length (norm)    -> sqrt(9+4) = 3.605...

# Cosine similarity: dot product, normalised. The ML workhorse.
cos = np.dot(a, b) / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b))
print(round(float(cos), 3))   # -> 0.857  (they point in fairly similar directions)

# Distance between two data points = norm of their difference
print(np.linalg.norm(a - b))  # -> 2.0

5. A matrix is a verb, not a noun

Most courses say "a matrix is a grid of numbers." True, but useless. The powerful idea, the one 3Blue1Brown will burn into your brain, is:

🔑 The big reframe

A matrix is a function that transforms space. Multiply a vector by a matrix and the vector gets rotated, stretched, squished, or sheared. The columns of the matrix tell you exactly where the basis arrows \(\hat{i}\) and \(\hat{j}\) land. Everything else follows.

Matrix–vector multiplication \(\mathbf{y} = A\mathbf{x}\) is just: "take \(\mathbf{x}\)'s coordinates, and use them to mix the columns of \(A\)." It's a linear combination of \(A\)'s columns. For example, the matrix that rotates everything 90° counter-clockwise sends \(\hat{i}=(1,0)\to(0,1)\) and \(\hat{j}=(0,1)\to(-1,0)\):

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad A\begin{bmatrix}3\\2\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}-2\\3\end{bmatrix} \]

The outer product \(\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^\top\) does the opposite of the dot product: it takes two vectors and builds a whole matrix (a rank-1 transformation). It's the atom that SVD (below) breaks matrices into.

6. Eigenvectors: the directions a transformation doesn't knock off course

When a matrix \(A\) transforms space, most arrows get knocked into a new direction. But a special few keep pointing exactly the same way — they only get stretched or shrunk. Those are the eigenvectors, and the stretch factor is the eigenvalue \(\lambda\):

\[ A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v} \]

Read it aloud: "applying the transformation \(A\) to \(\mathbf{v}\) does the same thing as simply scaling \(\mathbf{v}\) by \(\lambda\)." Eigenvectors are the skeleton of a transformation — its natural axes.

🌀 Real-World Analogy

Spin a globe. Almost every city moves. But the two poles stay put — they're on the axis of rotation. The rotation axis is the eigenvector of "spinning the Earth." Find a transformation's eigenvectors and you've found the directions along which it behaves most simply.

Eigendecomposition rebuilds the whole matrix from its eigen-skeleton: \(A = Q\Lambda Q^{-1}\), where \(Q\) holds the eigenvectors and \(\Lambda\) is a diagonal matrix of eigenvalues. This is exactly the engine of PCA (Week 20): the eigenvectors of a data set's covariance matrix point along the directions of greatest variation — the data's "natural axes."

7. Let's actually find them — a full worked example

Formulas feel like magic spells until you turn the crank once by hand. So let's take a real matrix and find its eigenvalues and eigenvectors step by step. We'll use the same matrix the code below uses, so you can check our hand-work against NumPy:

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \]

First, decode the symbols

Three pieces of notation scare people off. Here's what each one actually means, in plain words:

  • \(I\) — the identity matrix \(\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\). It's the "do-nothing" matrix: multiply any vector by \(I\) and the vector comes out unchanged (it's the number 1 of the matrix world). So \(\lambda I = \begin{bmatrix}\lambda&0\\0&\lambda\end{bmatrix}\) just means "the number \(\lambda\) on the diagonal."
  • \(A - \lambda I\) — take \(A\) and subtract \(\lambda\) from each diagonal entry. That's it. \(A-\lambda I = \begin{bmatrix} 2-\lambda & 1 \\ 1 & 2-\lambda \end{bmatrix}\).
  • \(\det(\,\cdot\,) = 0\) — the determinant equals zero. For a 2×2 matrix \(\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}\), the determinant is simply \(ad - bc\), one number. Geometrically the determinant is the area-scaling factor of the transformation. When it's 0, the matrix squashes 2-D space flat onto a line — it collapses a whole dimension. And that collapse is the only way a non-zero vector \(\mathbf{v}\) can be sent all the way to the zero vector.
🧒 Explain Like I'm 10

We're hunting for special arrows that \(A\) only stretches. Rewrite \(A\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{v}\) as \(A\mathbf{v}-\lambda\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}\), i.e. \((A-\lambda I)\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}\). We want a real arrow \(\mathbf{v}\) (not the zero arrow) that this new matrix crushes down to nothing. A matrix can only crush a real arrow to zero if it "flattens" space — and the flatten-detector is the determinant being 0. So we first find the \(\lambda\) values that make \(\det(A-\lambda I)=0\). Those are the eigenvalues.

Step 1 — Build the characteristic equation \(\det(A-\lambda I)=0\)

Plug in and compute the determinant \(ad-bc\) with \(a=d=2-\lambda\) and \(b=c=1\):

\[ \det\!\begin{bmatrix} 2-\lambda & 1 \\ 1 & 2-\lambda \end{bmatrix} = (2-\lambda)(2-\lambda) - (1)(1) = (2-\lambda)^2 - 1 = 0 \]

Step 2 — Solve it for \(\lambda\) (these are the eigenvalues)

\[ (2-\lambda)^2 = 1 \;\Longrightarrow\; 2-\lambda = \pm 1 \;\Longrightarrow\; \boxed{\lambda_1 = 3,\quad \lambda_2 = 1} \]

Two eigenvalues: 3 and 1. They tell us this transformation stretches along one special direction by ×3 and along another by ×1. Now we find which directions.

Step 3 — For each \(\lambda\), find its eigenvector by solving \((A-\lambda I)\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}\)

For \(\lambda_1 = 3\): subtract 3 from the diagonal and ask which \(\mathbf{v}=\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}\) gets crushed to zero:

\[ (A-3I)\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}0\\0\end{bmatrix} \]

The top row reads \(-x + y = 0\), i.e. \(y = x\). (The bottom row says the same thing — that redundancy is the determinant being zero, showing up as promised.) So any arrow with \(y=x\) works. Pick the simplest, \(\mathbf{v}_1=\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}\), pointing diagonally up-right.

For \(\lambda_2 = 1\): subtract 1 from the diagonal:

\[ (A-1I)\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}0\\0\end{bmatrix} \;\Rightarrow\; x+y=0 \;\Rightarrow\; y=-x \]

So \(\mathbf{v}_2=\begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\end{bmatrix}\), pointing diagonally down-right — exactly perpendicular to \(\mathbf{v}_1\).

Step 4 — Normalise (make them length 1)

Eigenvectors only care about direction, so we usually scale them to length 1 using the norm from section 4 (\(\lVert[1,1]\rVert=\sqrt{2}\)):

\[ \hat{\mathbf{v}}_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}\approx\begin{bmatrix}0.707\\0.707\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{v}}_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\end{bmatrix}\approx\begin{bmatrix}0.707\\-0.707\end{bmatrix} \]

And that's the whole recipe. Notice this matches the NumPy output in the code box below exactly: eig returns eigenvalues [3, 1] and those same two diagonal directions. You just did by hand what the library does.

Why the information lives in that direction

Here's the payoff — and the reason this matters for the rest of AI. Suppose \(A\) isn't a random matrix but a covariance matrix: a little summary of how a cloud of data points spreads out. (You'll build covariance matrices properly in Week 4 and Week 20; for now just know each entry measures how two features vary together.) A symmetric matrix like our \(A\) is exactly the shape a covariance matrix takes.

For a covariance matrix, the eigenvalue is the amount of variance — the amount of spread, and therefore the amount of information — along its eigenvector's direction. Our data spreads 3 units along \(\hat{\mathbf{v}}_1\) (the up-right diagonal) but only 1 unit along \(\hat{\mathbf{v}}_2\). So the data is a stretched, tilted ellipse: long and informative along \(\hat{\mathbf{v}}_1\), thin and boring along \(\hat{\mathbf{v}}_2\).

🔑 The big "aha"

If you had to throw away one of the two directions to save space, you'd drop \(\hat{\mathbf{v}}_2\) (variance 1) and keep \(\hat{\mathbf{v}}_1\) (variance 3) — losing almost nothing. That single act — keep the high-eigenvalue directions, discard the low ones — is Principal Component Analysis (Week 20). Eigenvectors hand you the data's own natural axes; eigenvalues rank them by how much information each one holds. That's why "the information is in that direction."

📸 Real-World Analogy

Photographing a pencil, you instinctively turn so its long axis faces you — that view tells you the most about it; the end-on view (a tiny dot) tells you almost nothing. The long axis is the top eigenvector; "how long it looks" is the eigenvalue. PCA is a camera that automatically rotates to the most informative angle.

8. SVD: the master decomposition

Singular Value Decomposition says any matrix \(M\) — even non-square — can be written as three simple steps: rotate, stretch, rotate:

\[ M = U\,\Sigma\,V^\top \]

\(V^\top\) rotates, \(\Sigma\) (diagonal "singular values") stretches each axis, \(U\) rotates again. The singular values tell you which directions carry the most information. Keep only the biggest few and you've compressed the matrix while losing almost nothing — this is the math behind image compression, noise removal, topic modelling, and recommendation systems (the famous Netflix prize was SVD on a users × movies matrix).

numpy — eigen & SVD
import numpy as np

A = np.array([[2., 1.],
              [1., 2.]])

# Eigen-decomposition: directions A only stretches (doesn't rotate)
vals, vecs = np.linalg.eig(A)
print(vals)            # eigenvalues  -> [3. 1.]
print(vecs)            # eigenvectors (columns)

# Verify A v = lambda v for the first eigenpair
v0 = vecs[:, 0]
print(np.allclose(A @ v0, vals[0] * v0))   # -> True

# SVD: works on ANY matrix, even non-square
M = np.array([[3., 1., 1.],
              [-1., 3., 1.]])
U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(M)
print(S)               # singular values, largest first -> importance of each direction

# Low-rank approximation: keep only the top singular value -> compression
M_approx = U[:, :1] @ np.diag(S[:1]) @ Vt[:1, :]
print(np.round(M_approx, 2))   # a compressed stand-in for M
