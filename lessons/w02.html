Linear Algebra: Vectors, Matrices & Eigen-everything

⏱️ 60 min🎯 The language of data📦 Week 2

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to…

  • See a vector two ways: as an arrow in space and as a list of data.
  • Add, scale, and measure vectors (norms) and find the angle between them (dot product).
  • Read a matrix as a machine that transforms space.
  • Understand eigenvectors/eigenvalues, eigendecomposition, and SVD — and why they power PCA, embeddings, and recommendation systems.
  • Do all of it in NumPy.
The Big Question
If every photo, word, and customer is just a list of numbers, what can we do with those lists to make patterns appear?

1. A vector is two things at once

In Lesson 1 we said "everything becomes a vector." Now let's actually meet one. A vector like \(\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\) has two equally true interpretations, and switching between them on demand is the single most useful skill in this course:

  1. An arrow in space — start at the origin, walk 3 right and 2 up. This is the geometric view. It makes "distance," "direction," and "angle" meaningful.
  2. A list of features — e.g. a house = [bedrooms, bathrooms] = [3, 2]. This is the data view.
🗺️ Real-World Analogy

A vector is like a treasure-map instruction: "3 paces east, 2 paces north." The instruction (the numbers) and the actual spot on the ground (the arrow) are the same information in two costumes. Data scientists wear the "list of numbers" costume; geometry wears the "arrow" costume. Pros switch costumes mid-sentence.

🧒 Explain Like I'm 10

A vector is just a list of numbers in a fixed order — like a recipe: [2 eggs, 1 cup flour, 3 spoons sugar]. Change the order and it's a different recipe. Computers love these lists because they can do maths on them. When we say "a cat photo is a vector," we just mean we wrote down a long list of numbers (the brightness of each dot) that stands for the picture.

2. The two operations that build everything: add & scale

You only ever do two fundamental things to vectors, and every fancy algorithm later is built from these:

Addition (tip-to-tail): \(\begin{bmatrix}3\\2\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}4\\4\end{bmatrix}\). Geometrically, walk the first arrow, then walk the second from where you landed.

Scaling (stretch/shrink): \(2 \cdot \begin{bmatrix}3\\2\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}6\\4\end{bmatrix}\). Same direction, twice as long. A negative scalar flips it around.

Combine these — "add up scaled copies of some vectors" — and you get a linear combination, the most important phrase in linear algebra. A neural network layer? A giant pile of linear combinations. Hold that thought until Week 24.

3. The dot product: how similar are two vectors?

The dot product takes two vectors and returns a single number that measures how much they point the same way:

\[ \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = \sum_i a_i b_i = a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + \dots = \lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert\cos\theta \]

  • Big positive → they point the same way (similar).
  • Zero → they're perpendicular (unrelated). This is huge: "uncorrelated" = "orthogonal."
  • Negative → they point opposite ways.
🔑 Why you'll never forget this

"How similar are two things?" is the question in machine learning — similar customers, similar documents, similar words. The dot product (and its cousin, cosine similarity) is the answer, and it shows up literally everywhere: in k-NN (Week 15), in SVMs (Week 18), and as the beating heart of attention in transformers (Week 32), where the model asks "how relevant is each word to this one?" with a dot product.

4. The norm: how long is a vector?

The norm \(\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert\) is the vector's length — straight from Pythagoras:

\[ \lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert_2 = \sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2 + \dots + v_n^2} = \sqrt{\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v}} \]

The distance between two data points \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\) is just \(\lVert\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}\rVert\). That one formula is how a model decides "these two houses are similar" or "this email looks like spam I've seen before."

numpy
import numpy as np

a = np.array([3, 2])
b = np.array([1, 2])

a + b              # vector addition  -> array([4, 4])
2 * a              # scaling          -> array([6, 4])
np.dot(a, b)       # dot product      -> 3*1 + 2*2 = 7
np.linalg.norm(a)  # length (norm)    -> sqrt(9+4) = 3.605...

# Cosine similarity: dot product, normalised. The ML workhorse.
cos = np.dot(a, b) / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b))
print(round(float(cos), 3))   # -> 0.857  (they point in fairly similar directions)

# Distance between two data points = norm of their difference
print(np.linalg.norm(a - b))  # -> 2.0

5. A matrix is a verb, not a noun

Most courses say "a matrix is a grid of numbers." True, but useless. The powerful idea, the one 3Blue1Brown will burn into your brain, is:

🔑 The big reframe

A matrix is a function that transforms space. Multiply a vector by a matrix and the vector gets rotated, stretched, squished, or sheared. The columns of the matrix tell you exactly where the basis arrows \(\hat{i}\) and \(\hat{j}\) land. Everything else follows.

Matrix–vector multiplication \(\mathbf{y} = A\mathbf{x}\) is just: "take \(\mathbf{x}\)'s coordinates, and use them to mix the columns of \(A\)." It's a linear combination of \(A\)'s columns. For example, the matrix that rotates everything 90° counter-clockwise sends \(\hat{i}=(1,0)\to(0,1)\) and \(\hat{j}=(0,1)\to(-1,0)\):

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad A\begin{bmatrix}3\\2\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}-2\\3\end{bmatrix} \]

The outer product \(\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^\top\) does the opposite of the dot product: it takes two vectors and builds a whole matrix (a rank-1 transformation). It's the atom that SVD (below) breaks matrices into.

6. Eigenvectors: the directions a transformation doesn't knock off course

When a matrix \(A\) transforms space, most arrows get knocked into a new direction. But a special few keep pointing exactly the same way — they only get stretched or shrunk. Those are the eigenvectors, and the stretch factor is the eigenvalue \(\lambda\):

\[ A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v} \]

Read it aloud: "applying the transformation \(A\) to \(\mathbf{v}\) does the same thing as simply scaling \(\mathbf{v}\) by \(\lambda\)." Eigenvectors are the skeleton of a transformation — its natural axes.

🌀 Real-World Analogy

Spin a globe. Almost every city moves. But the two poles stay put — they're on the axis of rotation. The rotation axis is the eigenvector of "spinning the Earth." Find a transformation's eigenvectors and you've found the directions along which it behaves most simply.

Eigendecomposition rebuilds the whole matrix from its eigen-skeleton: \(A = Q\Lambda Q^{-1}\), where \(Q\) holds the eigenvectors and \(\Lambda\) is a diagonal matrix of eigenvalues. This is exactly the engine of PCA (Week 20): the eigenvectors of a data set's covariance matrix point along the directions of greatest variation — the data's "natural axes."

7. SVD: the master decomposition

Singular Value Decomposition says any matrix \(M\) — even non-square — can be written as three simple steps: rotate, stretch, rotate:

\[ M = U\,\Sigma\,V^\top \]

\(V^\top\) rotates, \(\Sigma\) (diagonal "singular values") stretches each axis, \(U\) rotates again. The singular values tell you which directions carry the most information. Keep only the biggest few and you've compressed the matrix while losing almost nothing — this is the math behind image compression, noise removal, topic modelling, and recommendation systems (the famous Netflix prize was SVD on a users × movies matrix).

numpy — eigen & SVD
import numpy as np

A = np.array([[2., 1.],
              [1., 2.]])

# Eigen-decomposition: directions A only stretches (doesn't rotate)
vals, vecs = np.linalg.eig(A)
print(vals)            # eigenvalues  -> [3. 1.]
print(vecs)            # eigenvectors (columns)

# Verify A v = lambda v for the first eigenpair
v0 = vecs[:, 0]
print(np.allclose(A @ v0, vals[0] * v0))   # -> True

# SVD: works on ANY matrix, even non-square
M = np.array([[3., 1., 1.],
              [-1., 3., 1.]])
U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(M)
print(S)               # singular values, largest first -> importance of each direction

# Low-rank approximation: keep only the top singular value -> compression
M_approx = U[:, :1] @ np.diag(S[:1]) @ Vt[:1, :]
print(np.round(M_approx, 2))   # a compressed stand-in for M
