The Big Question
k-NN had no model of the world — it just looked at neighbours. What if, instead, we reasoned like a detective: "given the evidence in this email, what's the probability it's spam?" How do we turn raw probability theory into a working classifier?

1. Bayes' theorem: updating belief with evidence

Week 4 gave us probability; now we put it to work. We want \(P(\text{class}\mid\text{data})\): given the features we observe, how likely is each class? Bayes' theorem flips a hard question into easy pieces:

\[ P(C \mid \mathbf{x}) = \frac{P(\mathbf{x}\mid C)\,P(C)}{P(\mathbf{x})} \]

  • \(P(C\mid\mathbf{x})\) — the posterior: what we want, the belief after seeing the data.
  • \(P(\mathbf{x}\mid C)\) — the likelihood: how typical this data is if the class were \(C\).
  • \(P(C)\) — the prior: how common the class is before we look.
  • \(P(\mathbf{x})\) — the evidence, the same for every class, so we can ignore it when comparing.

So the rule a Bayes classifier follows is: pick the class with the largest posterior, i.e. the largest likelihood × prior.

\[ \hat{C} = \arg\max_{C} \; P(\mathbf{x}\mid C)\,P(C) \qquad (\text{posterior} \propto \text{likelihood}\times\text{prior}) \]

🧒 Explain Like I'm 10

You hear a bark outside. Is it a dog or a fox? Dogs bark a lot (high likelihood) and there are tons of dogs around (high prior), while foxes rarely bark and are rare anyway. So you bet "dog." Bayes' theorem is just doing this maths carefully: combine "how normal is this clue for each animal" with "how common is each animal."

2. Maximum likelihood estimation: where the numbers come from

To use the likelihood \(P(\mathbf{x}\mid C)\) we need its actual shape. Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) answers: given the data we saw, which parameter values make that data most probable? For a Gaussian with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\), MLE gives the comforting answer that the best \(\mu\) is just the sample mean and the best \(\sigma^2\) is the sample variance:

\[ \hat{\mu} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i, \qquad \hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\hat{\mu})^2 \]

MLE is the quiet workhorse behind almost every fitted model in this course — including, secretly, the least-squares regression of Week 13.

3. A Bayes classifier with a Gaussian (unimodal) likelihood

The most common recipe assumes each class's features follow a bell curve. The one-dimensional Gaussian density is:

\[ P(x\mid C) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_C^2}}\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu_C)^2}{2\sigma_C^2}\right) \]

So for each class we estimate its mean and variance with MLE, then for a new point evaluate every class's bell curve, multiply by the prior, and take the winner. With two well-separated bells the boundary lands neatly where the curves cross.

4. Summary so far

A Bayes classifier is generative: it learns what each class looks like (its probability distribution) and then asks Bayes' theorem which class most likely produced the new point. If our distribution assumptions are right, it is provably the best possible classifier. The catch arrives with many features.

5. Naïve Bayes: a wild simplification that works

With \(d\) features, the full likelihood \(P(x_1,\dots,x_d\mid C)\) is a monstrous joint distribution we can never estimate from finite data. Naïve Bayes makes one bold assumption — that the features are conditionally independent given the class — so the joint factorises into a simple product:

\[ P(x_1,\dots,x_d\mid C) \approx \prod_{i=1}^{d} P(x_i\mid C) \]

It's called "naïve" because that assumption is almost always false — in real emails the words "free" and "money" are obviously correlated. Yet it works astonishingly well, especially for text and spam, because even when the probabilities are off, the ranking of classes usually stays right, and the product is cheap to estimate from word counts.

🕵️ Real-World Analogy

Naïve Bayes is a detective who treats every clue as if it were unrelated to the others. "Muddy boots? Slightly points to the gardener. A grudge? Slightly points to the gardener. Was near the scene? Same." It pretends the clues don't overlap and just stacks up the votes. Sloppy logic — but pile up enough independent-ish clues and the verdict is usually right.

Naïve Bayes by hand: a spam filter in numbers

The formula above feels abstract until you push real numbers through it. Let's build a tiny spam filter from scratch. Suppose we've already read 10 training emails — 5 labelled spam, 5 labelled ham (not spam) — and we only ever look at three words. Here is every time each word appeared:

WordCount in spamCount in ham
free41
money31
meeting16
total words88

Step 1 — the prior. Half the training emails were spam, so before reading a single word: \(P(\text{spam}) = \tfrac{5}{10} = 0.5\) and \(P(\text{ham}) = 0.5\).

Step 2 — the per-word likelihoods. For each word we just divide its count by the total words in that class — that's the MLE from section 2, applied to word frequencies:

\[ P(\text{free}\mid\text{spam}) = \tfrac{4}{8} = 0.50, \qquad P(\text{money}\mid\text{spam}) = \tfrac{3}{8} = 0.375 \]

\[ P(\text{free}\mid\text{ham}) = \tfrac{1}{8} = 0.125, \qquad P(\text{money}\mid\text{ham}) = \tfrac{1}{8} = 0.125 \]

Step 3 — a new email arrives: "free money". The "naïve" step is right here: we assume the two words are independent given the class, so we just multiply their likelihoods together with the prior (the product \(\prod_i P(x_i\mid C)\) from the formula above):

\[ \text{score}_{\text{spam}} = \underbrace{0.5}_{\text{prior}} \times \underbrace{0.50}_{\text{free}} \times \underbrace{0.375}_{\text{money}} = 0.09375 \]

\[ \text{score}_{\text{ham}} = \underbrace{0.5}_{\text{prior}} \times \underbrace{0.125}_{\text{free}} \times \underbrace{0.125}_{\text{money}} = 0.0078125 \]

Step 4 — pick the winner and read off the probability. Spam scores about 12× higher, so we classify the email as spam. To turn the two scores into an actual posterior probability, just normalise them so they sum to 1 (this is the \(P(\mathbf{x})\) denominator we'd been ignoring):

\[ P(\text{spam}\mid\text{"free money"}) = \frac{0.09375}{0.09375 + 0.0078125} \approx 0.92 \]

So the filter is 92% sure this email is spam — computed with nothing but counting and multiplying. That is the entire algorithm.

⚠️ The zero-frequency trap (and its one-line fix)

Now an email says "free lottery", but the word "lottery" never appeared in any ham email. Then \(P(\text{lottery}\mid\text{ham}) = \tfrac{0}{8} = 0\), and because we multiply, that single zero annihilates the whole ham score — one unseen word vetoes everything else. Real text always has unseen words, so this happens constantly.

The fix is Laplace (add-one) smoothing: pretend you saw every word one extra time. Add 1 to every count and add the vocabulary size \(V\) to each denominator, so \(P(w\mid C) = \frac{\text{count}+1}{\text{total}+V}\). No probability is ever exactly zero, and scikit-learn's GaussianNB/MultinomialNB do this for you by default.

scikit-learn — GaussianNB
import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import make_classification
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.naive_bayes import GaussianNB
from sklearn.metrics import classification_report

# Toy 2-class data with 4 features
X, y = make_classification(n_samples=300, n_features=4, n_informative=3,
                           n_redundant=0, random_state=1)
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.30, random_state=1)

# Naive Bayes assumes each feature is an independent Gaussian per class.
nb = GaussianNB()
nb.fit(X_tr, y_tr)                      # MLE for each class's mean & variance

print("class priors  P(C):", np.round(nb.class_prior_, 3))
print(classification_report(y_te, nb.predict(X_te), digits=2))
# predict_proba gives the posterior P(C | x) — the Bayes belief, per class.
print("posterior of first 3 test points:")
print(np.round(nb.predict_proba(X_te[:3]), 3))

6. Gaussian Mixture Models: when one bell isn't enough

A single Gaussian is unimodal — it has one peak. But real data is often multimodal: think "heights of all adults," which has two humps (one per typical sex). A Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) describes such data as a weighted sum of \(K\) Gaussians:

\[ p(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k \,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}\mid \mu_k, \Sigma_k), \qquad \sum_k \pi_k = 1 \]

Each \(\pi_k\) is the weight ("how much of the population belongs to bump \(k\)"). We fit GMMs with the Expectation–Maximisation (EM) algorithm, which alternates between guessing which bump each point came from and re-fitting the bumps — a soft, probabilistic cousin of clustering.

7. A Bayes classifier using GMMs

Here's the payoff: replace each class's single Gaussian likelihood with a GMM. Now a class can have several clusters — "spam" might split into pharmacy-spam and lottery-spam — and the Bayes rule still applies: fit a GMM per class, then assign each new point to the class whose mixture (× prior) gives it the highest density. It is the flexible, multimodal upgrade of section 3.

🔑 The one thing to remember

Bayes classifiers are generative: they model how each class produces data (its distribution), then invert with Bayes' theorem. Naïve Bayes makes them cheap by pretending features are independent; GMMs make them flexible by allowing many bumps per class.

✅ Check Yourself

"Posterior ∝ likelihood × prior" means a Bayes classifier picks the class that…
Since P(x) is the same for every class, comparing posteriors reduces to comparing likelihood × prior. The class that best explains the data and is common enough wins.
Why is Naïve Bayes called "naïve"?
The conditional-independence assumption lets the joint likelihood factor into a product of per-feature terms. It's wrong in reality (words correlate) but keeps estimation tractable and the class ranking accurate.
You have data with two clear humps (bimodal). The right tool is…
One Gaussian is unimodal and would smear across the gap. A GMM places one component on each hump and learns their weights via EM.
