🧮 Part 4 · Classical Machine Learning

Regression II: Nonlinear, Overfitting & Regularisation

⏱️ 55 min🎯 The most important practical idea in ML📦 Week 14

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to…

  • Fit curves, not just lines, with polynomial regression.
  • Diagnose underfitting vs overfitting and explain the bias–variance tradeoff.
  • See train error and test error diverge as model complexity grows.
  • Tame overfitting with regularisation — specifically ridge (L2) regression.
  • Apply all of this to polynomial hypersurface fitting.
The Big Question
A wiggly curve that threads through every training point perfectly sounds like the best possible model. So why does it often make the worst predictions on new data?

1. When a line isn't enough: nonlinear regression

Last week we fit straight lines and flat hyperplanes. But plenty of relationships curve — temperature over a day, the path of a thrown ball, diminishing returns on advertising. A straight line through curved data will always miss. We need models that bend.

The cleverest trick is also the laziest: keep linear regression's machinery, but feed it new features made from the old ones. To fit a curve in \(x\), add \(x^2\), \(x^3\), and so on as extra columns. The model stays linear in its weights (so the normal equation still works) but becomes a curve in \(x\). This is polynomial regression:

\[ \hat{y} = w_0 + w_1 x + w_2 x^2 + \dots + w_d x^d \]

The degree \(d\) is a dial for model complexity. Degree 1 is a line; degree 2 a parabola; high degrees can wiggle wildly. And therein lies the whole drama of this lesson.

2. Underfitting, good fit, overfitting

Turn the complexity dial and three regimes appear:

  • Underfitting (too simple): a degree-1 line on clearly curved data. It can't capture the trend; it's wrong on both training and new data. We say it has high bias.
  • Good fit (just right): the model captures the real trend and ignores the random noise. Low error on training and new data.
  • Overfitting (too complex): a high-degree curve that snakes through every training point, chasing the noise. Near-zero error on training, but it lurches wildly between points and predicts terribly on new data. We say it has high variance.
🧒 Explain Like I'm 10

Two kids study for a maths test. One memorises the exact answers to last year's practice questions — they ace the practice but flop on the real test because the questions changed. The other understands how the maths works — they do fine on anything. Overfitting is memorising the practice answers. Good learning is understanding the pattern. We always want understanding.

🔑 The bias–variance tradeoff

Total prediction error splits into bias (error from being too simple to capture the truth) plus variance (error from being so flexible you fit the noise), plus irreducible noise. Simpler models: high bias, low variance. Complex models: low bias, high variance. The sweet spot is in the middle. This single tradeoff governs every model in the rest of the course.

3. The tell-tale sign: train error vs test error

How do you detect overfitting without a crystal ball? Split your data: train on one part, evaluate on a held-out test set the model never saw. Then watch both errors as complexity rises:

  • Training error falls steadily — more flexibility always fits the seen data better, all the way to zero.
  • Test error falls at first (escaping underfitting), bottoms out at the sweet spot, then rises as the model starts memorising noise.

That widening gap — tiny train error, growing test error — is the unmistakable fingerprint of overfitting.

4. The cure: regularisation & ridge regression

Overfitting in polynomial models shows up as enormous weights — the curve needs giant, opposing coefficients to make those violent wiggles. Regularisation fights back by adding a penalty for large weights right into the loss, so the model has to earn any complexity it uses. Ridge regression (a.k.a. L2 regularisation) adds the sum of squared weights:

\[ J(\mathbf{w}) = \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(y_i - \hat{y}_i\bigr)^2}_{\text{fit the data}} \;+\; \underbrace{\alpha \sum_{j=1}^{d} w_j^2}_{\text{stay simple}} \]

The knob \(\alpha \ge 0\) sets the strength. At \(\alpha=0\) it's plain regression. Crank \(\alpha\) up and the penalty shrinks the weights toward zero, smoothing the curve and trading a little bias for a big drop in variance. Beautifully, ridge keeps a closed-form solution — just add \(\alpha\) to the diagonal before inverting:

\[ \mathbf{w}^\star = (X^\top X + \alpha I)^{-1} X^\top \mathbf{y} \]

🎚️ Real-World Analogy

Regularisation is a "complexity tax." Every unit of wiggle a model wants to add costs it something, so it only buys the wiggles that genuinely reduce error and leaves the noise-chasing flourishes on the shelf. Raise the tax (\(\alpha\)) and the model gets simpler and calmer; drop it to zero and the model is free to overfit.

5. Seeing it happen in code

Let's fit polynomials of growing degree to noisy data, watch train and test error diverge, then rescue the wiggly degree-15 model with ridge.

scikit-learn — overfitting & ridge
import numpy as np
from sklearn.preprocessing import PolynomialFeatures
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression, Ridge
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.metrics import mean_squared_error
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split

# True signal is a gentle sine; we only see noisy samples of it
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
X = np.sort(rng.uniform(0, 1, 60)).reshape(-1, 1)
y = np.sin(2*np.pi*X).ravel() + rng.normal(0, 0.2, 60)
Xtr, Xte, ytr, yte = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.4, random_state=0)

# Fit increasing polynomial degrees; print train vs test error
print("degree | train MSE | test MSE")
for deg in [1, 3, 9, 15]:
    pipe = make_pipeline(PolynomialFeatures(deg), LinearRegression()).fit(Xtr, ytr)
    tr = mean_squared_error(ytr, pipe.predict(Xtr))
    te = mean_squared_error(yte, pipe.predict(Xte))
    print(f"  {deg:>4} |  {tr:7.3f}  |  {te:7.3f}")
# As degree rises, train MSE keeps dropping but test MSE balloons -> OVERFITTING

# Now regularise the wiggly degree-15 model with ridge (L2 penalty)
ridge = make_pipeline(PolynomialFeatures(15), Ridge(alpha=1e-2)).fit(Xtr, ytr)
print("ridge deg15 test MSE:", round(mean_squared_error(yte, ridge.predict(Xte)), 3))
# The alpha penalty shrinks the huge weights -> test error drops back down

Run it and you'll see the unregularised test MSE explode at high degree while training MSE shrinks toward zero — the signature of overfitting. Switching on ridge tames the degree-15 model, pulling its test error back down without touching the polynomial degree. Same flexibility, but penalised into behaving.

6. Polynomial hypersurfaces & the takeaway

Everything here generalises beyond one feature. With several inputs you can add cross-terms like \(x_1 x_2\) and squares \(x_1^2\), fitting a curved hypersurface instead of a flat hyperplane. More features and higher degrees mean more flexibility — and more chances to overfit — so regularisation becomes more essential, not less, as dimensionality grows.

⚠️ Watch out

Never tune \(\alpha\) or the polynomial degree on your test set — that leaks test information and quietly overfits to it. Use a separate validation set or cross-validation (e.g. RidgeCV). Keep the test set in a sealed envelope, opened once, for the final honest score.

🔗 Connect the Dots

Overfitting and regularisation are not a regression footnote — they are the central tension of all machine learning, and they recur in every later model. Decision trees overfit until you prune them (Week 21); the bias–variance tradeoff is exactly why we average many trees into a random forest (Week 22). And the L2 penalty you just met returns as weight decay and its cousin dropout for neural networks (Week 27) — same idea, same goal: stop the model from memorising the noise. ✅ Check Yourself

A model gets near-zero error on training data but high error on new test data. This is…
Acing the training set but failing on unseen data is the textbook signature of overfitting: the model memorised the noise rather than learning the underlying pattern.
How does ridge (L2) regularisation reduce overfitting?
Ridge adds \(\alpha\sum w_j^2\) to the loss, so large weights are costly. The model shrinks its coefficients, smoothing the curve and trading a little bias for a big drop in variance.
As you increase a polynomial model's degree, what typically happens to training error?
More flexibility always fits the seen data better, so training error falls monotonically toward zero. Test error, however, makes a U — and the gap between them reveals overfitting.
